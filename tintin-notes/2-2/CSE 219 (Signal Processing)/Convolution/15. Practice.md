
# Module 15: Convolution Practice Toolkit — Accumulator Trick, Splitting, and Overlap Regions

This module isn't new theory — it's three _reusable techniques_ for handling convolutions that are too "infinite" or "combination-shaped" to just grind through with a table like Module 6's finite example. Master these three, and CT2 Q1 becomes routine instead of scary.

## Technique 1: Convolving with $u[n]$ = "running total" (the accumulator trick)

**The single most useful shortcut in this whole toolkit.** Whenever you convolve _any_ signal $x[n]$ with the unit step $h[n]=u[n]$, something special happens:

$$y[n] = x[n]*u[n] = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} x[k],u[n-k]$$

Since $u[n-k]=1$ exactly when $k\le n$, and $0$ otherwise, this sum collapses to:

$$y[n] = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{n} x[k]$$

**In plain English: convolving with $u[n]$ just computes the running (cumulative) sum of $x$ up to time $n$.** This is worth memorizing as a pattern, not re-deriving every time — it will save you enormous amounts of work on an exam.

### Worked example (Ex 2.3): $x[n]=\alpha^n u[n]$, $h[n]=u[n]$, $0<\alpha<1$

Since $x[n]$ is zero before $n=0$, the running sum for $n\ge 0$ is: $$y[n] = \sum_{k=0}^{n}\alpha^k = 1+\alpha+\alpha^2+\cdots+\alpha^n = \frac{1-\alpha^{n+1}}{1-\alpha}$$ (finite geometric series formula), and $y[n]=0$ for $n<0$ (nothing to accumulate yet). As $n\to\infty$, this approaches $\frac{1}{1-\alpha}$ — the _total_ accumulated sum, since the geometric series converges.

**Intuition:** think of $\alpha^n u[n]$ as a fading input arriving one sample at a time — money deposited into a bank account each day, where each day's deposit is smaller than the last. $y[n]=u[n]*x[n]$ is literally your **running bank balance** — it climbs quickly at first (big deposits) and levels off as the deposits shrink toward nothing.

### Worked example (Ex 2.5): $x[n]=2^n u[-n]$, $h[n]=u[n]$

Here $x[n]$ is nonzero only for $n\le 0$ (it's $1$ at $n=0$, $0.5$ at $n=-1$, $0.25$ at $n=-2$, etc. — decaying as you move _left_, away from $n=0$).

**Case $n\ge 0$:** the running sum captures _all_ of $x$'s nonzero values (all of them sit at $k\le 0 \le n$): $$y[n] = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{0} 2^k = \cdots + \tfrac14+\tfrac12+1 = 2$$ (a convergent geometric series summing backward — total accumulated value is 2, forever, once you've passed all of $x$'s support).

**Case $n<0$:** the running sum only captures the part of $x$ up to $k=n$: $$y[n] = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{n} 2^k = 2^{n+1}$$ (same backward-geometric-series logic, just truncated earlier).

**Intuition:** this time the "deposits" all happened _before_ time 0 and have already mostly finished by the time we start watching — so the running balance is already partway built up by $n<0$, and simply locks in at its final total ($2$) once we've passed $n=0$ and there's nothing left to add.

### Worked example (Ex 2.6, continuous-time version): $x(t)=e^{-at}u(t)$, $h(t)=u(t)$, $a>0$

Same idea, but the "running sum" becomes a **running integral**: $$y(t) = \int_{0}^{t} e^{-a\tau},d\tau = \frac{1}{a}\left(1-e^{-at}\right), \quad t>0; \qquad y(t)=0,\ t<0$$

This is the continuous-time accumulator: $y(t)$ climbs from $0$ toward the limiting value $\frac{1}{a}$ as $t\to\infty$ — exactly analogous to Example 2.3's discrete version climbing toward $\frac{1}{1-\alpha}$.

## Technique 2: The distributive property + "split and conquer"

This is the property that unlocks **any** signal made of two different formulas glued together (e.g., "one formula for $n\ge$ something, a different formula for $n<$ something") — which is _exactly_ the shape of both Example 2.10 and your actual CT2 exam question.

**The property itself** (a direct consequence of the linearity of the convolution sum — nothing new to prove, just apply Module 2's additivity to the sum): $$x[n]*\big(h_1[n]+h_2[n]\big) = x[n]*h_1[n] + x[n]*h_2[n]$$ $$\big(x_1[n]+x_2[n]\big)*h[n] = x_1[n]*h[n] + x_2[n]*h[n]$$

**Why this matters practically:** if $x[n]$ is a _sum_ of two "nice" pieces (each individually a clean one-sided exponential you already know how to convolve, using Technique 1), you don't need any new machinery — split $x$ into its two pieces, convolve each piece separately, then just **add the two results together.**

### Worked example (Ex 2.10): $x[n] = \left(\tfrac12\right)^n u[n] + 2^n u[-n]$, $h[n]=u[n]$

Notice this $x[n]$ is written with _two different formulas glued together_ — a right-sided decaying exponential ($n\ge 0$) plus a left-sided decaying exponential ($n\le 0$). This is precisely the situation the distributive property is built for.

**Split:** $x_1[n] = \left(\tfrac12\right)^n u[n]$ (right-sided piece), $x_2[n]=2^n u[-n]$ (left-sided piece).

**Convolve each separately** (both are problems we already solved above!):

- $y_1[n] = x_1[n]*u[n]$ — this is Example 2.3 with $\alpha=\frac12$: $y_1[n] = \dfrac{1-(1/2)^{n+1}}{1-1/2} = 2-2^{-n}$, for $n\ge 0$; $0$ otherwise.
- $y_2[n] = x_2[n]*u[n]$ — this is exactly Example 2.5: $y_2[n]=2^{n+1}$ for $n<0$; $y_2[n]=2$ for $n\ge 0$.

**Add:** $$y[n] = y_1[n]+y_2[n] = \begin{cases} 2^{n+1}, & n<0 \ (2-2^{-n})+2 = 4-2^{-n}, & n\ge 0 \end{cases}$$

**This is exactly the technique CT2 Question 1 wants from you** — a signal built from a left-sided piece plus a right-sided piece, each convolved separately against $h[n]$, then added. We'll drill this directly in Module 17.

## Technique 3: Two genuinely one-sided signals convolved together — the "overlap window" method

Sometimes _both_ signals are one-sided but in "opposing directions" — one only nonzero for $t\le 0$, the other only nonzero for $t\ge 0$ — and neither is simply "$u(t)$," so Technique 1's shortcut doesn't directly apply. Here you go back to first principles (Module 6's logic) but reason about the overlap region algebraically rather than sliding a picture.

### Worked example: $x(t) = e^t u(-t)$, $h(t) = e^{-t}u(t)$

**Step 1 — find where each factor is "alive":** $x(\tau) = e^\tau u(-\tau)$ is nonzero only for $\tau \le 0$. $h(t-\tau) = e^{-(t-\tau)}u(t-\tau)$ is nonzero only for $t-\tau \ge 0$, i.e., $\tau \le t$.

**Step 2 — find the overlap:** both conditions must hold simultaneously: $\tau\le 0$ **and** $\tau \le t$. This means $\tau \le \min(0,t)$ — the overlap's upper limit depends on whether $t$ is negative or positive, which is _why_ this problem naturally splits into two cases.

**Step 3 — write the product on the overlap:** $x(\tau)h(t-\tau) = e^\tau \cdot e^{-(t-\tau)} = e^{-t}e^{2\tau}$.

**Case $t<0$:** overlap is $\tau \in (-\infty, t]$: $$y(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{t} e^{-t}e^{2\tau},d\tau = e^{-t}\left[\tfrac12 e^{2\tau}\right]_{-\infty}^{t} = e^{-t}\cdot\tfrac12 e^{2t} = \tfrac12 e^{t}$$

**Case $t\ge 0$:** overlap is $\tau\in(-\infty,0]$ (since $\min(0,t)=0$ now): $$y(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{0} e^{-t}e^{2\tau},d\tau = e^{-t}\cdot\tfrac12 = \tfrac12 e^{-t}$$

**Final answer:** $$y(t) = \begin{cases}\tfrac12 e^t, & t<0 \\ \tfrac12 e^{-t}, & t\ge 0\end{cases}$$

Notice the result is a symmetric two-sided decaying exponential (peaking at $t=0$, decaying identically in both directions) — this shape shows up constantly in signals/probability (it's essentially a Laplace-distribution shape) and is worth recognizing on sight.

## The meta-skill: how to recognize which technique applies

|Signal shape you're given|Technique to reach for|
|---|---|
|One signal is exactly $u[n]$ or $u(t)$|**Technique 1** — running sum/integral, use the geometric series formula directly|
|$x[n]$ (or $h[n]$) is a _sum_ of two differently-shaped pieces (e.g. "one thing for $n\ge0$, another for $n<0$")|**Technique 2** — split via linearity, convolve each piece separately, add results|
|Both signals are one-sided in _opposite_ directions, neither is a plain step|**Technique 3** — find the overlap region as a function of $t$ (or $n$), likely producing case-split boundaries at $\min$/$\max$ of endpoints|
|Combination of the above (e.g., a split signal _and_ the other signal isn't a plain step)|Apply Technique 2 first to split, **then** Technique 3 (or direct summation) on each piece separately|

That last row is _exactly_ what CT2 Question 1 needs — we'll build up to it directly in Module 17.

**2.** Split: $x_1[n] = 3\left(\tfrac12\right)^n u[n]$, $x_2[n] = 4^n u[-n]$.

$y_1[n] = x_1[n]*u[n] = 3\cdot\left[\dfrac{1-(1/2)^{n+1}}{1-1/2}\right] = 3\left(2-2^{-n}\right) = 6-3\cdot 2^{-n}$, for $n\ge 0$ (using Ex 2.3's template, scaled by the constant 3, since convolution is linear — scaling $x$ scales $y$ by the same factor).

$y_2[n] = x_2[n]*u[n]$, using Ex 2.5's template with base 4 instead of 2: for $n\ge0$: $y_2[n] = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{0}4^k = \cdots+\tfrac{1}{16}+\tfrac14+1 = \dfrac{1}{1-1/4} = \dfrac43$ (geometric series with ratio $1/4$, summed backward). For $n<0$: $y_2[n] = \sum_{k=-\infty}^n 4^k = \dfrac{4^{n+1}}{1-1/4}\cdot$... let's redo carefully: $\sum_{k=-\infty}^{n} 4^k$. Let $j=-k$ (so $j$ ranges from $-n$ to $\infty$): $=\sum_{j=-n}^{\infty} 4^{-j} = \sum_{j=-n}^\infty (1/4)^j = \dfrac{(1/4)^{-n}}{1-1/4} = \dfrac{4^{n}}{3/4} = \dfrac{4\cdot 4^n}{3} = \dfrac{4^{n+1}}{3}$.

So $y_2[n] = \dfrac{4^{n+1}}{3}$ for $n<0$; $y_2[n]=\dfrac43$ for $n\ge0$.

**Total:** $$y[n] = y_1[n]+y_2[n] = \begin{cases} \dfrac{4^{n+1}}{3}, & n<0 \\ \left(6-3\cdot2^{-n}\right)+\dfrac43 = \dfrac{22}{3}-3\cdot2^{-n}, & n\ge 0\end{cases}$$

**3.** $x(\tau)=e^{-\tau}u(\tau)$ is nonzero for $\tau\ge 0$. $h(t-\tau) = e^{2(t-\tau)}u(-(t-\tau)) = e^{2(t-\tau)}u(\tau-t)$ is nonzero for $\tau \ge t$.

**Overlap condition:** need $\tau\ge 0$ **and** $\tau\ge t$ simultaneously, i.e. $\tau \ge \max(0,t)$.

Product on overlap: $x(\tau)h(t-\tau) = e^{-\tau}\cdot e^{2(t-\tau)} = e^{2t}e^{-3\tau}$.

**Case $t<0$:** $\max(0,t)=0$, overlap is $\tau\in[0,\infty)$: $$y(t) = \int_0^\infty e^{2t}e^{-3\tau},d\tau = e^{2t}\left[-\tfrac13e^{-3\tau}\right]_0^\infty = e^{2t}\cdot\tfrac13 = \tfrac13 e^{2t}$$

**Final answer:** $$y(t) = \begin{cases}\tfrac13 e^{2t}, & t<0\ \tfrac13 e^{-t}, & t\ge 0\end{cases}$$

(Same symmetric-decaying-exponential _shape_ as the worked example, but with an asymmetric decay rate on each side — since the two original signals decayed at different rates, $e^{-\tau}$ vs. $e^{2\tau}$ — a good reminder that the overlap-region method handles mismatched rates just fine, it just changes the numbers, not the method.)

**4.** Technique 1's shortcut relies on $h[n]$ being _exactly_ $u[n]$ — a plain "switch on at $n=0$, stay on forever, height always exactly 1" signal, which is precisely what makes the convolution sum collapse into "just add up all of $x$ up to time $n$." But $h[n]=\left(\tfrac12\right)^n u[n-2]$ is **not** a plain step — it switches on two steps _later_ ($n=2$ instead of $n=0$) **and** it isn't constant height 1 afterward — it decays. Since the "multiplier" attached to each term in the running sum is no longer uniformly 1, you can't just blindly sum $x[k]$ values; you'd actually be computing $\sum_k x[k]\left(\tfrac12\right)^{n-k}u[n-k-2]$, which is a genuinely different (weighted, delay-shifted) sum, not a plain running total.

The correct strategy: since $h[n]$ is a shifted, decaying exponential rather than a plain step, you'd want to either (a) treat this as a direct convolution using the reflect-shift-multiply-add / overlap-region reasoning (Technique 3's spirit, adapted to discrete time), tracking the "switch-on" delay of 2 explicitly in your index bookkeeping, or (b) if $x[n]$ itself is also built from multiple pieces, use Technique 2 to split $x[n]$ first, then handle each piece against this shifted-decaying $h[n]$ individually. **This exact scenario — a decaying exponential kernel that only switches on after a delay — is precisely the shape of your actual CT2 Question 1's $h[n]$**, which is exactly why we're building this recognition skill now.

## First: let's get $x[n] = 2^n u[-n]$ absolutely straight

You said "won't all the stuff for $n>0$ be zero?" — **you are completely correct about that part.** Let's confirm with real numbers where $x[n]$ actually lives.

$u[-n]$ equals $1$ when $-n \ge 0$, i.e., when $n \le 0$. So $x[n]$ is nonzero **only for $n \le 0$**, and yes, $x[n] = 0$ for every $n > 0$. Let's tabulate actual values:

|$n$|$-3$|$-2$|$-1$|$0$|$1$|$2$|$3$|
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
|$x[n]$|$2^{-3}=0.125$|$2^{-2}=0.25$|$2^{-1}=0.5$|$2^0=1$|$0$|$0$|$0$|

So $x[n]$ is a **left-sided signal**: it lives entirely in the region $n\le 0$, it's _largest_ right at $n=0$ (value $1$), and it fades away as you go further left (more negative $n$). You had it right — it's zero for $n>0$. Good.

## The part that's tripping you up: $y[n] \ne x[n]$

Here's the key thing to internalize: *_$y[n]=x[n]_u[n]$ is a completely different signal from $x[n]$ itself.__ The fact that $x[n]$ is zero for $n>0$ does **not** mean $y[n]$ has to be zero there too. Why? Because of what convolving-with-$u[n]$ _means_, as we established in Technique 1:

$$y[n] = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{n} x[k] \quad \text{— "add up everything } x \text{ has done, from the beginning of time, up through time } n\text{"}$$

This is a **running total / accumulator**, like a bank balance. Once money has been deposited, it stays in the account even after the deposits stop. The balance doesn't reset to zero just because _today's_ deposit happens to be zero — it just stops growing and holds steady at whatever total was already reached.

## Walking through $y[n]$ step by step, with real numbers

Let's literally compute $y[n] = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{n} x[k]$ at a few actual values of $n$, using the table above, so you can _see_ the accumulator in action.

**At $n=-2$:** add up all $x[k]$ for $k \le -2$: $y[-2] = \cdots + x[-4]+x[-3]+x[-2] = \cdots+0.03125+0.0625+0.125+0.25 = 0.5$ (Check against the formula $y[n]=2^{n+1}$: $2^{-2+1}=2^{-1}=0.5$ ✓)

**At $n=-1$:** add up all $x[k]$ for $k\le -1$: $y[-1] = \cdots+x[-2]+x[-1] = 0.5+0.25+0.125+\cdots = 1$ (Check: $2^{-1+1}=2^0=1$ ✓ — notice this is exactly $y[-2]$ plus the one new deposit $x[-1]=0.5$: $0.5+0.5=1$ ✓)

**At $n=0$:** add up all $x[k]$ for $k\le 0$ — **this now includes every single nonzero value $x[n]$ ever has**, since $x$'s entire nonzero content lives at $k\le 0$: $$y[0] = \cdots+x[-2]+x[-1]+x[0] = \cdots+0.25+0.5+1 = 2$$ (This is the full geometric series $1+0.5+0.25+0.125+\cdots = \dfrac{1}{1-0.5}=2$ — check: our case-2 formula also gives $y[0]=2$ directly.)

**At $n=1$:** add up all $x[k]$ for $k\le 1$. But $x[1]=0$! So this adds _nothing new_ compared to $n=0$: $$y[1] = y[0] + x[1] = 2+0 = 2$$

**At $n=2,3,4,\dots$:** same story — every new term being added is $x[2]=x[3]=\cdots=0$, so the running total just **stays frozen at 2, forever.**

## So here's the resolution to your exact question

> "won't all the stuff $n>0$ be zero?"

**$x[n]$ is zero for $n>0$ — yes.** But $y[n]$ isn't "the stuff at $n>0$" — it's "everything accumulated _up to and including_ $n$." By the time $n$ reaches $0$, the accumulator has _already_ swallowed every nonzero value $x$ ever had (since $x$'s support ends at $n=0$ and never comes back). So from $n=0$ onward, there's simply nothing left to add — the total just sits at its final value, $2$, like a bank balance after all deposits have stopped.

**The "2" isn't mysterious — it's just the sum of the entire geometric series $1+\frac12+\frac14+\frac18+\cdots$, which equals exactly $2$.** That's the total amount of "stuff" $x[n]$ ever contained, and once you've swept past all of it, that's the number you're left holding.

## Why the _other_ example (Ex 2.3, $\alpha^n u[n]$) does the opposite thing

You asked: "if this is 2, why does the previous one give 0 for $n<0$?" — good instinct to compare them, because they're mirror images of each other, and seeing _why_ they're opposite is the real lesson.

$x[n] = \alpha^n u[n]$ is a **right-sided** signal — nonzero only for $n\ge 0$ (opposite of Example 2.5's left-sided signal). Table:

|$n$|$-2$|$-1$|$0$|$1$|$2$|$3$|
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
|$x[n]$|$0$|$0$|$1$|$\alpha$|$\alpha^2$|$\alpha^3$|

Now run the _same_ accumulator logic:

**For $n<0$** (say $n=-1$): $y[-1] = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{-1} x[k]$. But every single $x[k]$ for $k\le -1$ is **zero** (the signal hasn't "started" yet — its support only begins at $k=0$). So there's genuinely nothing to add up: $y[n]=0$ for all $n<0$.

**For $n\ge 0$:** now we start sweeping _into_ the signal's support for the first time, and each new $n$ picks up one more nonzero term: $y[0]=1$, $y[1]=1+\alpha$, $y[2]=1+\alpha+\alpha^2$, and so on — actively growing, approaching $\frac{1}{1-\alpha}$ as $n\to\infty$ but (unlike Example 2.5) never _quite_ finishing, since this signal's support stretches infinitely to the _right_, so the sweep never fully completes.

**The two examples are exact mirror images:** one signal's "mass" sits entirely to the left of $n=0$ (so the running sum is _already finished accumulating_ by the time $n\ge 0$, and is _zero_ before that), the other's mass sits entirely to the right of $n=0$ (so the running sum _hasn't started_ accumulating for $n<0$, and is still _actively growing_ for $n\ge0$).

## The general, confidence-building recipe for _any_ $x[n]*u[n]$ problem

Follow these steps, in order, every single time, and you'll never get confused again:

1. **Find the support of $x[n]$** — where is it nonzero? Is it left-sided (like $\cdots u[-n]$, stretching to $-\infty$), right-sided (like $\cdots u[n]$, stretching to $+\infty$), or finite (nonzero only over some finite range)?
    
2. **Picture $n$ as a "sweeping boundary" moving left to right along the number line.** The running sum $y[n]$ only ever includes the part of $x[k]$ that sits _at or to the left of_ this boundary ($k\le n$).
    
3. **Ask: has the sweeping boundary $n$ reached the support of $x$ yet?**
    
    - If $n$ is still _entirely to the left_ of all of $x$'s nonzero content → nothing accumulated yet → $y[n]=0$.
    - If $n$ is _inside_ $x$'s nonzero region (partway through) → partial sum, changing with $n$ (this is where the actual geometric-series algebra happens).
    - If $n$ has swept _completely past_ all of $x$'s nonzero content (and $x$ has no more nonzero values further right) → the sum has captured _everything_ → constant final value, locked forever after.
4. **The specific case-split boundary in your formula ($n<0$ vs. $n\ge 0$) is always exactly wherever $x[n]$'s support starts or ends** — that's not a coincidence, it's _forced_ by step 3's logic. For a left-sided $x$ ending at $n=0$: the "swept past everything" case is $n\ge 0$. For a right-sided $x$ starting at $n=0$: the "hasn't started yet" case is $n<0$.

# How to Solve CT2 Q1-Type Problems

Your CT2 Question 1 has exactly the "combination signal convolved with a delayed kernel" shape that's genuinely the hardest pattern in this whole course — so let's build one more technique, then give you a bulletproof step-by-step algorithm, then execute it fully on your actual exam question.

## Technique 4 (new): pull the delay out first, convolve the "clean" pieces, then shift back in

This is a shortcut that will save you a _lot_ of algebra whenever $h[n]$ (or $x[n]$) contains a built-in delay like $u[n-2]$ instead of a plain $u[n]$.

**The property:** if $h[n] = g[n-n_0]$ (i.e., $h$ is just some simpler signal $g$, shifted right by $n_0$), then: $$x[n]*h[n] = x[n]_g[n-n_0] = \big(x_g\big)[n-n_0]$$

**Proof (one line):** $(x_h)[n]=\sum_k x[k]h[n-k]=\sum_k x[k]g[(n-k)-n_0] = \sum_k x[k]g[(n-n_0)-k] = (x_g)[n-n_0]$.

**Why this is useful:** it lets you convolve against the _undelayed_, simpler version $g[n]$ (which is usually just a plain one-sided exponential you already know how to handle with Technique 1's style of summation) and then just relabel $n\to n-n_0$ at the very end — instead of dragging the awkward "$-n_0$" through every step of the summation.

## The general algorithm for "split-signal convolved with delayed kernel" problems

1. **Split $x[n]$ into its separately-defined pieces** (left-sided part, right-sided part) using the distributive property (Technique 2).
2. **Identify the support (valid index range) of each piece**, and of $h[n]$ — write down the exact inequality defining where each is nonzero.
3. **If $h[n]$ has a built-in shift** (like $u[n-2]$), factor it as $h[n]=g[n-n_0]$ for a simpler $g$, so you can use Technique 4.
4. **Convolve each piece of $x$ against $h$ (or against $g$, then shift) separately**, using direct summation: find the valid range of the summation index $k$ (intersection of both signals' support conditions — this is where most errors happen, so do it carefully and explicitly, never skip it), then evaluate the geometric series.
5. **Add all the pieces back together**, being careful that each piece is only "active" (nonzero) over its own specific range of $n$ — your final answer will usually be a piecewise formula with 2 or 3 cases.

The single most error-prone step is #4's index-range intersection — this is exactly where I made (and caught) an arithmetic slip below, so let's walk through it slowly.

$$x[n] = 4^n u[-n-3] + \left(\tfrac14\right)^n u[n], \qquad h[n] = \left(\tfrac12\right)^n u[n-2]$$

### Step 1 — Split $x[n]$

$$x_1[n] = 4^n u[-n-3] \quad(\text{left-sided}), \qquad x_2[n] = \left(\tfrac14\right)^n u[n] \quad(\text{right-sided})$$

By linearity: $y[n] = x_1[n]*h[n] + x_2[n]*h[n] = y_1[n]+y_2[n]$.

### Step 2 — Find the support of each signal

- $x_1[n]$: $u[-n-3]=1 \iff -n-3\ge0 \iff n\le -3$.
- $x_2[n]$: $u[n]=1 \iff n\ge 0$.
- $h[n]$: $u[n-2]=1 \iff n\ge 2$.

### Computing $y_2[n] = x_2[n]*h[n]$ first (the easier piece)

$$y_2[n] = \sum_k x_2[k]h[n-k] = \sum_k \left(\tfrac14\right)^k u[k]\cdot\left(\tfrac12\right)^{n-k}u[n-k-2]$$

**Valid range of $k$:** need $k\ge 0$ **and** $n-k-2\ge 0$ (i.e., $k\le n-2$). So $k$ runs from $0$ to $n-2$ — this requires $n\ge2$, or the range is empty.

$$y_2[n] = \sum_{k=0}^{n-2}\left(\tfrac14\right)^k\left(\tfrac12\right)^{n-k} = \left(\tfrac12\right)^n\sum_{k=0}^{n-2}\left(\tfrac14\right)^k\left(2\right)^k = \left(\tfrac12\right)^n\sum_{k=0}^{n-2}\left(\tfrac12\right)^k$$

Using the finite geometric sum: $\sum_{k=0}^{n-2}(1/2)^k = 2\left[1-(1/2)^{n-1}\right]$:

$$y_2[n] = \left(\tfrac12\right)^n\cdot 2\left[1-\left(\tfrac12\right)^{n-1}\right] = 2\left(\tfrac12\right)^n - \left(\tfrac12\right)^{2n-2} = \left(\tfrac12\right)^{n-1} - \left(\tfrac14\right)^{n-1}$$

$$\boxed{y_2[n] = \left(\tfrac12\right)^{n-1}-\left(\tfrac14\right)^{n-1}, \quad n\ge 2; \qquad 0 \text{ otherwise}}$$

_(Sanity check via Technique 4: write $h[n]=g[n-2]$ with $g[n]=\tfrac14\left(\tfrac12\right)^n u[n]$. Then $x_2 *g$ at general index $m$ works out to $(1/2)^{m+1}-(1/4)^{m+1}$, and shifting by 2 gives exactly $(1/2)^{n-1}-(1/4)^{n-1}$ — confirms the answer independently.)

### Computing $y_1[n] = x_1[n]*h[n]$ (the harder piece)

$$y_1[n] = \sum_k 4^k u[-k-3]\cdot\left(\tfrac12\right)^{n-k}u[n-k-2]$$

**Valid range of $k$:** need $k\le -3$ **and** $k \le n-2$ simultaneously — i.e., $k \le \min(-3,, n-2)$.

This minimum flips depending on $n$, which is _why_ the answer needs two cases:

**Case A: $n\ge -1$** (so $n-2\ge -3$, meaning $-3$ is the binding/smaller limit): $k$ ranges over $(-\infty, -3]$.

$$y_1[n] = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{-3} 4^k\left(\tfrac12\right)^{n-k} = \left(\tfrac12\right)^n\sum_{k=-\infty}^{-3}8^k$$

Substituting $k=-3-m$ ($m=0,1,2,\dots$): $\sum_{k=-\infty}^{-3}8^k = 8^{-3}\sum_{m=0}^\infty (1/8)^m = 8^{-3}\cdot\tfrac{8}{7} = \tfrac{1}{448}$.

$$y_1[n] = \frac{1}{448}\left(\tfrac12\right)^n, \qquad n\ge -1$$

**Case B: $n<-1$** (so $n-2<-3$, meaning $n-2$ is now the binding limit): $k$ ranges over $(-\infty,, n-2]$.

$$\sum_{k=-\infty}^{n-2}8^k = 8^{n-2}\cdot\tfrac87 = \frac{8^{n-1}}{7}$$

$$y_1[n] = \left(\tfrac12\right)^n\cdot\frac{8^{n-1}}{7} = \frac{4^n}{56}, \qquad n<-1$$

$$\boxed{y_1[n] = \begin{cases}\dfrac{4^n}{56}, & n\le -2\\ \dfrac{1}{448}\left(\tfrac12\right)^n, & n\ge -1\end{cases}}$$

### Step 5 — Add everything together

Since $y_2[n]$ only "switches on" at $n\ge 2$, while $y_1[n]$ covers _every_ integer already:

$$y[n] = \begin{cases} \dfrac{4^n}{56}, & n\le -2 \\ \dfrac{1}{448}\left(\tfrac12\right)^n, & -1\le n\le 1 \\ \dfrac{1}{448}\left(\tfrac12\right)^n + \left(\tfrac12\right)^{n-1}-\left(\tfrac14\right)^{n-1}, & n\ge 2 \end{cases}$$

That's the complete answer. Notice it required _every single technique_ from Modules 15–17: splitting (Technique 2), direct geometric-series summation with careful index-range intersection (the core Module 6 skill), and the shift-out property (Technique 4) as a cross-check. This really is the "boss level" of the whole convolution unit — if you can reproduce this from scratch, you're in excellent shape for the CT.

## Practice — smaller versions of the same pattern (with full answers)

**1.** $x[n] = \left(\tfrac13\right)^n u[n]$, $h[n] = \left(\tfrac12\right)^n u[n-1]$. Find $y[n]=x[n]*h[n]$, using the range-intersection method (there should be just **one** case here, not two — no left-sided piece to split off).

**2.** $x[n] = 2^n u[-n-1] + u[n]$, $h[n]=\delta[n]-\delta[n-1]$ (a simple finite kernel, not exponential — this tests whether you can apply the _splitting_ idea even when $h$ isn't an exponential). Find $y[n]$.

---

## Answers

**1.** Single signal, no splitting needed. $y[n]=\sum_k \left(\tfrac13\right)^k u[k]\left(\tfrac12\right)^{n-k}u[n-k-1]$. Valid range: $k\ge0$ and $k\le n-1$, so $0\le k\le n-1$ (requires $n\ge1$).

$$y[n] = \sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\left(\tfrac13\right)^k\left(\tfrac12\right)^{n-k} = \left(\tfrac12\right)^n\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\left(\tfrac23\right)^k = \left(\tfrac12\right)^n\cdot 3\left[1-\left(\tfrac23\right)^n\right]$$

$$y[n] = 3\left(\tfrac12\right)^n - 3\left(\tfrac23\right)^n\left(\tfrac12\right)^n = 3\left(\tfrac12\right)^n - 3\left(\tfrac13\right)^n, \quad n\ge1;\qquad 0 \text{ otherwise}$$

**2.** Split $x[n] = x_a[n]+x_b[n]$ where $x_a[n]=2^n u[-n-1]$ (support $n\le -1$) and $x_b[n]=u[n]$ (support $n\ge0$).

Since $h[n]=\delta[n]-\delta[n-1]$ is a simple 2-term kernel, convolving with it just means: $y[n] = x[n]-x[n-1]$ for _any_ $x$ (a first-difference — no geometric series needed at all here).

$y_a[n] = x_a[n]-x_a[n-1] = 2^n u[-n-1] - 2^{n-1}u[-n]$. For $n\le -2$: both terms active, $=2^n - 2^{n-1}=2^{n-1}$. At $n=-1$: $x_a[-1]=2^{-1}u[0]=0.5$, $x_a[-2]=2^{-2}u[1]=0$ (since $u[-(-1)-1]=u[0]=1$ so $x_a[-1]=2^{-1}$; and $x_a[n-1]$ at $n=-1$ means $x_a[-2]=2^{-2}u[-(-2)-1]=2^{-2}u[1]=0.25$). Let me just tabulate directly instead: $x_a[n]=2^n$ for $n\le-1$, else 0. So $y_a[n]=x_a[n]-x_a[n-1]$: for $n\le-2$: $2^n-2^{n-1}=2^{n-1}$. At $n=-1$: $x_a[-1]-x_a[-2]=2^{-1}-2^{-2}=0.25$. At $n=0$: $x_a[0]-x_a[-1]=0-2^{-1}=-0.5$. For $n\ge1$: $0-0=0$.

$y_b[n]=x_b[n]-x_b[n-1]=u[n]-u[n-1]=\delta[n]$ (a step minus its own delayed copy is just an impulse — this is a nice standalone fact worth remembering).

**Total:** $y[n]=y_a[n]+y_b[n]$: for $n\le-2$: $2^{n-1}$; at $n=-1$: $0.25$; at $n=0$: $-0.5+1=0.5$; for $n\ge1$: $0$.

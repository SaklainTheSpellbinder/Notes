# Module 7: Properties of Convolution — Commutativity

## The core claim

$$a * b = b * a$$

In words: **it doesn't matter which of the two sequences you call "the signal" and which you call "the impulse response" (or "kernel") — the convolution comes out identical either way.** This might seem like a throwaway algebraic footnote, but it actually carries real conceptual weight for how you're allowed to _think_ about convolution, so let's dig into both the _proof_ and the _intuition_.

## Why it's true: the polynomial-multiplication argument

Recall from Module 5: convolution was born from asking "what's the coefficient of $z^n$ in $A(z)B(z)$?" But ordinary multiplication of numbers (and hence of polynomials) is commutative — you learned in grade school that $3 \times 5 = 5 \times 3$, and this extends completely to polynomials:

$$A(z)\cdot B(z) = B(z)\cdot A(z)$$

This is just a re-statement of a fact you've known forever: multiplication order doesn't matter. Since convolution _is_ the coefficient-extraction operation applied to a product of polynomials, and since swapping which polynomial you call "$A$" and which you call "$B$" obviously doesn't change the _product_ $A(z)B(z)$ at all (it's the same polynomial either way!) — the coefficients of that product, i.e., the convolution result, **must** also be unchanged. There's no room for the two orderings to disagree, because they're literally computing the coefficients of the exact same object.

## Why it's true: the direct algebraic proof (the rigorous version)

You actually already did the heavy lifting for this in Module 5, Exercise 4 — let's connect the dots explicitly. Start from the definition:

$$(a*b)[n] = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} a[k],b[n-k]$$

Now perform a substitution: let $k' = n-k$, which means $k = n-k'$. As $k$ ranges over all integers (from $-\infty$ to $\infty$), $k'=n-k$ _also_ ranges over all integers — just in reverse order, which doesn't affect the value of an infinite sum (you're adding up the exact same collection of terms, just listing them in a different sequence). Substituting:

$$\sum_{k} a[k],b[n-k] = \sum_{k'} a[n-k'],b[k']$$

Relabeling the dummy variable $k'$ back to $k$ (dummy variable names are just placeholders — renaming one never changes what a sum equals):

$$= \sum_k a[n-k],b[k] = \sum_k b[k],a[n-k] = (b*a)[n]$$

So $(a_b)[n] = (b_a)[n]$ for every $n$ — proven directly from the summation definition, no polynomial analogy required. This confirms the polynomial argument wasn't just a hand-wavy analogy; it's backed by a clean one-line substitution proof.

## The intuition: two equally valid ways to picture the _same_ calculation

Here's where commutativity becomes more than a technicality — it means **you get to choose which sequence you reflect-and-slide**, and both choices give identical output.

Picture 1 (from the slides — the "penguin and shark" image): keep $x[n]$ fixed and stationary, reflect $h[n]$, and slide the reflected $h$ rightward across the fixed $x$.

Picture 2 (the flipped version): keep $h[n]$ fixed and stationary instead, reflect $x[n]$, and slide the reflected $x$ rightward across the fixed $h$.

Both pictures produce the exact same sequence of output values $y[n]$. This matters practically: sometimes one of your two sequences is much shorter or algebraically simpler to reflect (e.g., a 2-sample kernel vs. a 50-sample signal) — commutativity tells you it's always safe to reflect _whichever one is more convenient_, and you'll never accidentally get a different (wrong) answer by making that choice.

## A worked confirmation, using numbers you already trust

Let's take the exact sequences from Module 6, but swap their roles, and confirm we land on the identical output.

Original: $x={1,2,1,1}$ ($n=0..3$), $h={1,1,-1,2}$ ($n=0..3$), and we computed $y[2] = 2$ by fixing $x$ and sliding reflected $h$.

Now let's instead fix $h$ and slide reflected $x$, to compute $(h*x)[2]$:

We need $\sum_k h[k],x[2-k]$, valid for $k$ such that $h[k]$ exists ($k\in{0,1,2,3}$) _and_ $x[2-k]$ exists ($2-k\in{0,1,2,3} \Rightarrow k \in {-1,0,1,2}$). Intersection: $k\in{0,1,2}$.

$$y[2] = h[0]x[2] + h[1]x[1] + h[2]x[0] = (1)(1) + (1)(2) + (-1)(1) = 1+2-1 = 2$$

**Identical to the $y[2]=2$ we got in Module 6**, computed the opposite way. This is commutativity, verified numerically rather than just asserted.

## An important word of caution (this is where students slip up)

Commutativity says $a_b = b_a$ — **it does not say anything about which sequence gets reflected being "arbitrary" in some sloppy sense.** You must still be internally consistent: if you decide to fix $a$ and slide reflected $b$, you must reflect _all of_ $b$ and slide _the whole thing_; you can't reflect only part of it, or reflect $a$ _and_ $b$ simultaneously (double-reflection would actually undo the flip and give you correlation, a related but genuinely _different_ operation — not covered in these slides, but worth knowing it exists so you don't confuse it with convolution on an exam). Commutativity is a statement about the _final answer_ being order-independent — it is not a license to be careless about correctly executing reflect-shift-multiply-add once you've picked an order.

## Answers

**1.** We want $(h*x)[n] = \sum_k h[k]x[n-k]$, with $h$ having valid indices ${0,1}$ and $x$ having valid indices ${0,1,2}$. Expected length: $2+3-1=4$, indices $n=0,1,2,3$ (matches previous result's range).

- $n=0$: need $k\in{0,1}$ and $-k\in{0,1,2}\Rightarrow k\in{-2,-1,0}$. Intersection: $k=0$. $y[0]=h[0]x[0]=(3)(2)=6$
- $n=1$: need $k\in{0,1}$ and $1-k\in{0,1,2}\Rightarrow k\in{-1,0,1}$. Intersection: $k\in{0,1}$. $y[1]=h[0]x[1]+h[1]x[0]=(3)(0)+(1)(2)=0+2=2$
- $n=2$: need $k\in{0,1}$ and $2-k\in{0,1,2}\Rightarrow k\in{0,1,2}$. Intersection: $k\in{0,1}$. $y[2]=h[0]x[2]+h[1]x[1]=(3)(-1)+(1)(0)=-3+0=-3$
- $n=3$: need $k\in{0,1}$ and $3-k\in{0,1,2}\Rightarrow k\in{1,2,3}$. Intersection: $k=1$. $y[3]=h[1]x[2]=(1)(-1)=-1$

Result: $(h_x)[n] = {6,2,-3,-1}$ — **identical** to $(x_h)[n]$ from Module 6, Exercise 1. ✓ Commutativity confirmed numerically.

**2.** This reasoning is wrong because it confuses **convolution** with **plain elementwise (Hadamard) multiplication**. Commutativity of convolution says the _entire sequence_ $(a_b)$ equals the _entire sequence_ $(b_a)$ — it says nothing about convolution collapsing into a simple product at matching indices; convolution involves _summing over all shifts_, not just one aligned position.

Counterexample: let $a={1,2}$ ($n=0,1$) and $b={3,4}$ ($n=0,1$). Term-by-term product would give (if the student's claim were true) something like ${a[0]b[0], a[1]b[1]} = {3, 8}$. But actual convolution:

- $(a*b)[0] = a[0]b[0] = 3$
- $(a*b)[1] = a[0]b[1]+a[1]b[0] = 4+6=10$
- $(a*b)[2] = a[1]b[1] = 8$

Real result: ${3,10,8}$ — a **length-3** sequence, not a length-2 elementwise product ${3,8}$. The middle term ($10$) doesn't even exist in the student's proposed shortcut, and the lengths themselves don't match (3 vs 2). This conclusively disproves the elementwise-multiplication shortcut.

**3.** Define $c[n] = \sum_{i+j=n} a_i b_j$, summed over all valid pairs $(i,j)$ with $0\le i\le N$, $0\le j \le M$, and $i+j=n$ (this is exactly the criss-cross coefficient of $z^n$ in $A(z)B(z)$).

Substitute $i' = n-i$ (so $i = n-i'$) wherever $i$ appears, keeping the constraint $i+j=n$ intact (equivalently $j = n-i$, so as $i$ ranges over its valid values, set $i'=j$, meaning we're just relabeling which index we call "the first" one):

$$c[n] = \sum_{i+j=n} a_ib_j = \sum_{j+i=n} b_ja_i$$

This is simply the same sum with the two factors written in the opposite order inside each term (using that $a_ib_j = b_ja_i$ for ordinary numbers) and the roles of "which index we call $i$" relabeled — but the sum $\sum_{j+i=n} b_j a_i$ is _by definition_ exactly the formula for the coefficient of $z^n$ in $B(z)A(z)$ (just swap which polynomial's coefficient we call $b$ vs $a$ in the original criss-cross derivation from Module 5). Since the constraint $i+j=n$ is symmetric in $i,j$ (it doesn't care which one you call "first"), the _same set of pairs_ gets summed either way — only the order of writing each individual product changes, which doesn't affect a numerical value. Hence coefficient of $z^n$ in $AB$ = coefficient of $z^n$ in $BA$. ✓

**4.** Yes — this is a genuinely useful strategic move, not just a theoretical curiosity. **Scenario:** suppose $x[n]$ is a long signal with 100 samples (indices $0$ to $99$), and $h[n]$ is a short 2-sample kernel (indices $0,1$) — e.g., a simple averaging filter $h={0.5, 0.5}$.

If you fix $x$ and reflect-and-slide $h$: at each output position $n$, you only ever need to check _at most 2_ candidate $k$-values (since $h$ only has 2 nonzero entries) — the valid-index-range bookkeeping is trivial (just "is $k=n$ in range of $x$? Is $k=n-1$ in range of $x$?").

If instead you tried to fix $h$ and reflect-and-slide the 100-sample $x$: you'd have to consider _up to 100_ candidate terms at each output position, checking each one's validity against the tiny 2-sample range of $h$ — far more bookkeeping overhead per output sample, even though the final answer would be identical by commutativity.

**Why this works:** the number of terms you must check at each step is governed by the length of the sequence you're sliding (reflecting), not the one you're keeping fixed — so it's always smarter to reflect-and-slide the **shorter** sequence, keeping the **longer** one fixed. This is exactly why, in practice (and in your Python lab), people always think of convolving a long signal with a _short_ impulse response/kernel, sliding the kernel across the signal — never the other way around — even though commutativity guarantees either order is mathematically valid.

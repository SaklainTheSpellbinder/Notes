# Module 13: Full Synthesis — "The Bridge"

## Why this module exists, even though we've technically already derived everything

Modules 9 through 12 built the convolution sum piece by piece, across four separate modules — and there's a real risk that, having gone through it that slowly, you now see it as four separate "topics" rather than one continuous argument. This module has exactly one job: **collapse everything back into a single, unified three-step mental model that you can recall in 10 seconds during your exam**, and explicitly show how it closes the loop back to the Module 8 "factors of 72" philosophy that started this whole chapter.

## The three-step bridge, stated cleanly

$$\boxed{1.\ \delta[n]\to h[n] \qquad 2.\ x[n]=\sum_k x[k]\delta[n-k] \qquad 3.\ y[n]=\sum_k x[k]h[n-k] = (x*h)[n]}$$

Let's restate what each step actually _buys_ you, in one sentence each, so the whole chain reads as a single coherent argument rather than three disconnected facts:

**Step 1 (Modules 9–10): Measure the system's reaction to the simplest possible signal.** You poke the LTI system once, with an impulse, and record what comes back. This is a _one-time_ experimental (or theoretical) act — you do this once, ever, for a given system, and you're done with measurements forever.

**Step 2 (Module 11): Recognize that _any_ future input, no matter how complicated, is secretly already just a big pile of scaled, shifted impulses.** This step requires _no new information about the system_ — it's a purely structural fact about signals in general, true regardless of what system you're dealing with.

**Step 3 (Module 12): Use linearity and time-invariance to convert your knowledge of "the response to one impulse" into "the response to every impulse in the pile," and then add those responses together.** This is where the two defining properties of LTI systems (and _only_ those two properties) get spent — nothing else about the system needs to be known.

## The direct mapping back to Module 8's "factors of 72" story

It's worth explicitly re-drawing the correspondence table now that all the pieces are filled in, since seeing it completed is genuinely clarifying:

|Factors-of-72 (Module 8)|Signals & LTI (Modules 9–12)|
|---|---|
|Break 72 into prime powers: $2^3\cdot 3^2$|Measure system's response to the atomic signal: $\delta[n]\to h[n]$|
|_Every_ integer is guaranteed a (unique) prime factorization|_Every_ signal is guaranteed a (unique) impulse decomposition: $x[n]=\sum_k x[k]\delta[n-k]$|
|Combine the per-prime counts via multiplication: $(3+1)(2+1)=12$|Combine the per-impulse responses via the convolution sum: $y[n]=\sum_k x[k]h[n-k]$|

The reason this correspondence isn't just a cute teaching device but a _genuine_ structural parallel: in both cases, the hard, seemingly-impossible problem ("how many factors does this specific big number have?" / "what does this system do to this specific complicated signal?") gets solved _without ever directly attacking the big/complicated object at all_. You solve a trivial sub-problem (counting choices per prime / finding the impulse response), lean on a guarantee that the complicated object decomposes cleanly into those trivial pieces with no exceptions, and then apply one clean combination rule. The complicated object is never tackled head-on — it's dissolved into pieces simple enough to have been solved already.

## The single most important thing to internalize: what convolution _is_, philosophically

After all thirteen modules, here's the one-sentence summary you should be able to produce cold, without hesitation, if asked "what is convolution and why do we care":

> **Convolution is the formula that tells you how to reconstruct an LTI system's output for _any_ input, using only the system's single impulse response — it exists because linearity lets you add up individual responses and time-invariance lets you shift a single known response to any location, and together those two facts are exactly enough to handle the fact that any signal is just a sum of shifted, scaled impulses.**

Every single piece of that sentence maps onto a specific module you've now worked through: "any input" ← Module 11's universal decomposition; "single impulse response" ← Module 10's definition; "linearity lets you add" ← Module 2 + Module 12 Step 4; "time-invariance lets you shift" ← Module 3 + Module 12 Step 2; "any signal is a sum of shifted scaled impulses" ← Module 11 again. Nothing in the formula is arbitrary — every symbol in $y[n]=\sum_k x[k]h[n-k]$ is traceable back to one specific piece of reasoning.

## A "final exam" style synthesis problem — working _forward and backward_ through the whole bridge

Let's do one problem that forces you to touch every step of the bridge in one go, since this is exactly the shape of question that tests whether you understand the _whole_ argument rather than isolated pieces.

**Problem:** An LTI system is measured to have impulse response $h[n] = {1, 2}$ at $n=0,1$ (zero elsewhere). You're given the input $x[n] = {3, -1, 2}$ at $n=0,1,2$ (zero elsewhere). Find $y[n]$ **using the full bridge explicitly** — don't jump straight to a convolution shortcut; show each of the three steps.

**Step 1 (given):** $\delta[n] \to h[n] = {1,2}$ at $n=0,1$.

**Step 2 — decompose $x[n]$:** $$x[n] = 3\delta[n] - \delta[n-1] + 2\delta[n-2]$$

**Step 3 — apply time-invariance + linearity to each piece, then add:**

By time-invariance: $\delta[n-1]\to h[n-1]$ (i.e., ${1,2}$ shifted to sit at $n=1,2$), and $\delta[n-2]\to h[n-2]$ (i.e., ${1,2}$ shifted to sit at $n=2,3$).

By linearity (scaling): $3\delta[n]\to 3h[n] = {3,6}$ at $n=0,1$. $-\delta[n-1]\to -h[n-1] = {-1,-2}$ at $n=1,2$. $2\delta[n-2]\to 2h[n-2] = {2,4}$ at $n=2,3$.

By linearity (additivity), align by index and sum:

|$n$|$3h[n]$|$-h[n-1]$|$2h[n-2]$|$y[n]$|
|---|---|---|---|---|
|0|3|0|0|**3**|
|1|6|$-1$|0|**5**|
|2|0|$-2$|2|**0**|
|3|0|0|4|**4**|

Final answer: $y[n] = {3, 5, 0, 4}$ for $n=0,1,2,3$. Length check: $N=3$ ($x$), $M=2$ ($h$), expected length $3+2-1=4$. ✓ Matches.

Notice: this is _exactly_ the reflect-shift-multiply-add mechanics of Module 6, just narrated through the "shift the known impulse response, scale it, add" language of Module 12 instead. Same numbers, same answer, two equivalent narrations — which is itself a nice confirmation of how tightly Modules 5–6 and 9–12 are secretly the same content viewed from two directions (bottom-up from polynomial algebra vs. top-down from LTI system properties).

## Exercises — Module 13 (with answers)

**1.** Fill in the blank conceptually (no computation): "If a system were merely _time-invariant_ but not linear, Step ___ of the bridge would still hold, but Step ___ would fail, because ___." Complete this sentence precisely, referencing the specific steps.

**2.** An LTI system has impulse response $h[n] = {2, 0, -1}$ at $n=0,1,2$. Input: $x[n] = {1, 1}$ at $n=0,1$. Carry out **all three steps of the bridge explicitly** (decompose $x$, shift/scale $h$, add) to find $y[n]$. State the expected output length first, then verify your answer matches it.

**3. (Conceptual, testing the "why not something simpler" angle.)** Suppose someone suggests: _"Why bother with impulses at all? Why not directly define $H$ = 'the system's response to the constant signal $x[n]=1$ for all $n$,' and use _that_ as the fundamental building block instead, since it's also a pretty simple signal?"_ Explain specifically why this alternative fails to reproduce the "any signal decomposes into copies of the building-block signal" property that made impulses special (Module 9's discussion) — i.e., show that you _cannot_ write an arbitrary signal $x[n]$ as a sum of scaled, shifted copies of the all-ones constant signal, the way you _can_ with impulses.

**4. (The bridge in reverse — a diagnostic problem.)** You're told an LTI system produces output $y[n] = {4, -2, 6}$ at $n=0,1,2$ when given input $x[n]={2}$ at $n=0$ (i.e., $x[n]=2\delta[n]$, a single scaled impulse, nothing else). Using only Steps 1 and the _scaling_ part of linearity (in reverse), determine the system's impulse response $h[n]$.

## Answers

**1.** "If a system were merely _time-invariant_ but not linear, **Step 2** of the bridge would still hold, but **Step 3** would fail, because Step 2 (the impulse decomposition of $x[n]$) is a purely structural fact about signals that doesn't depend on the system at all — it's always true. But Step 3 requires _both_ time-invariance (to shift the known response, which this hypothetical system does have) _and_ linearity (to scale each shifted response and then validly add all the pieces back together) — and without linearity, there's no guarantee the actual output for the combined signal equals the sum of the individually-shifted responses, so the final convolution formula would not correctly predict $y[n]$." _(This directly echoes Module 12, Exercise 3's reasoning — Step 1 measurement and Step 2 decomposition survive, but the final combination step breaks.)_

**2.** Expected output length: $x$ has $N=2$ samples, $h$ has $M=3$ samples, so expected length $= 2+3-1=4$, indices $n=0,1,2,3$.

**Step 2 — decompose:** $x[n] = 1\cdot\delta[n] + 1\cdot\delta[n-1]$.

**Step 3 — shift, scale, add:**

By time-invariance: $\delta[n]\to h[n]={2,0,-1}$ at $n=0,1,2$. $\delta[n-1]\to h[n-1] = {2,0,-1}$ shifted to $n=1,2,3$.

By linearity (scaling, both coefficients are 1, so no change in magnitude): contributions are ${2,0,-1}$ at $n=0,1,2$ and ${2,0,-1}$ at $n=1,2,3$.

By linearity (additivity):

|$n$|1st copy|2nd copy|$y[n]$|
|---|---|---|---|
|0|2|0|**2**|
|1|0|2|**2**|
|2|$-1$|0|**$-1$**|
|3|0|$-1$|**$-1$**|

$y[n] = {2, 2, -1, -1}$ for $n=0,1,2,3$ — length 4, matches the expected $(N+M-1)=4$. ✓

**3.** The key property that made $\delta[n]$ special (Module 9's discussion, reinforced in Module 11) was: _shifted copies of the impulse, $\delta[n-k]$, are each "active" at exactly one isolated point $n=k$ and silent everywhere else — so stacking them lets you set each sample of $x[n]$ independently, without any interference between different points._

The all-ones constant signal does **not** have this property. A shifted copy of the constant signal, $\mathbb{1}[n-k]$ (the constant signal "1" doesn't even meaningfully change under a shift — it's $1$ at every $n$ regardless of $k$!), is active _everywhere_, not just at one isolated point. If you tried to write $x[n] = \sum_k c[k]\cdot 1$ (some combination of scaled constant signals), every single term in the sum contributes to _every single index_ $n$ simultaneously — there's no way to isolate control over just one sample without affecting all the others. Concretely: try to build $x[n] = {5, 0, 0}$ (nonzero only at $n=0$) using scaled copies of the all-ones signal — you'd need coefficients $c[k]$ such that $\sum_k c[k] = 5$ at $n=0$ but $\sum_k c[k]=0$ at $n=1$ and $n=2$ — but since every copy of the constant signal contributes the _same_ value at _every_ $n$ (there's no shifting freedom that changes _where_ it's active, since a shifted constant signal is identical to the unshifted one), you cannot independently control the sum's value at different $n$'s using only scaled copies of "1." This confirms impulses are essential precisely _because_ of their pointwise isolation — no other simple "building-block" signal (constant, step, sinusoid) has this property in the same clean, exception-free way.

**4.** We're told $x[n] = 2\delta[n] \to y[n] = {4,-2,6}$ at $n=0,1,2$.

By linearity's scaling property (used in reverse): if $2\delta[n] \to y[n]$, and scaling the input by $2$ scales the output by $2$ (compared to the response to a _single_, unscaled impulse), then the response to the unscaled impulse $\delta[n]$ alone must be $y[n]$ divided by $2$: 
$$ h[n] = \frac{y[n]}{2} = \{ \frac{4}{2}, \frac{-2}{2}, \frac{6}{2} \} = {2, -1, 3} $$

So $h[n] = {2,-1,3}$ at $n=0,1,2$. _(Sanity check via forward direction: $2\cdot h[n] = 2\cdot{2,-1,3} = {4,-2,6}$ ✓, matches the given $y[n]$.)_

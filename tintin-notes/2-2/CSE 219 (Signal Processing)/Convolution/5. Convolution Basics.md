# Module 5: Convolution — Origins and Definition

## Setting the stage: convolution is just an operation on two sequences

Before connecting convolution to systems at all, the slides deliberately introduce it as a **pure, abstract operation** — something you can define and compute on two lists of numbers, with zero reference to signals or systems. This is a smart pedagogical choice, and it's worth understanding _why_: convolution shows up in polynomial algebra, probability (sums of independent random variables), image processing, and signal processing — it's the same operation wearing different costumes. Learning it "in the abstract" first means you're not tempted to think it's some mysterious signals-only trick; it's ordinary algebra.

Given two sequences $a[k]$ and $b[k]$, there are (at least) three things you might do to combine them:

- **Add**, position by position: $(a+b)[k] = a[k] + b[k]$
- **Subtract**, position by position: $(a-b)[k] = a[k] - b[k]$
- **Convolve** — a fundamentally different kind of mixing, denoted $(a*b)[k]$

Addition and subtraction are simple: line up the two sequences at matching indices, combine each pair. Convolution does _not_ work that way — instead of combining values at the _same_ index, it **mixes across all combinations of indices**, and _that's_ precisely what makes it new and worth an entire lecture.

## Where convolution actually comes from: multiplying polynomials

This is the cleanest way to _derive_ convolution rather than just being told the formula — and it's exactly how the slides build it, so let's make sure the mechanism is airtight.

Take two polynomials: $$A(z) = 1 + 3z + 2z^2 + 5z^3 + 4z^4 \qquad (a_0=1, a_1=3, a_2=2, a_3=5, a_4=4)$$ $$B(z) = 2 - z + 4z^2 + z^3 + 3z^4 \qquad (b_0=2, b_1=-1, b_2=4, b_3=1, b_4=3)$$

**Question:** if you multiply $A(z)\cdot B(z)$ out completely, what is the coefficient sitting in front of $z^4$?

### Why the coefficient of $z^4$ requires _criss-crossed_ pairs

When you multiply two polynomials, every single term of $A$ multiplies every single term of $B$ — you get a big pile of products like $a_0b_0$ (giving $z^0$), $a_1b_0$ (giving $z^1$), $a_2 b_1$ (giving $z^3$), and so on. Each product $a_i z^i \cdot b_j z^j$ contributes a term proportional to $z^{i+j}$.

If you want the coefficient of $z^4$ specifically, you need to gather up **every single pair $(i,j)$ whose powers add up to exactly 4** — because those are the only products that land in the $z^4$ "bucket." Since $i+j=4$ can happen many different ways ($i=0,j=4$; $i=1,j=3$; $i=2,j=2$; $i=3,j=1$; $i=4,j=0$), you must collect _all five_ of these pairs and add their products together:

$$c[4] = a_0 b_4 + a_1 b_3 + a_2 b_2 + a_3 b_1 + a_4 b_0$$

Plugging in numbers: $c[4] = 1(3) + 3(1) + 2(4) + 5(-1) + 4(2) = 3+3+8-5+8 = 17$.

Notice the pattern in the indices: as $i$ increases by 1 (going $0,1,2,3,4$), $j$ _decreases_ by 1 (going $4,3,2,1,0$) to keep the sum fixed at 4. This is the "criss-cross" — one index climbs while the other descends, always meeting at the same total. This is _exactly_ the geometric picture in the slides: draw $a_0,a_1,a_2,a_3,a_4$ in a row, $b_0,b_1,b_2,b_3,b_4$ in a row underneath, then draw diagonal lines connecting $a_i$ to $b_{4-i}$ for each $i$ — those diagonal connections are the pairs you sum.

### Generalizing: the compact formula

For a general power $z^n$, the exact same reasoning gives:

$$c[n] = \sum_k a[k], b[n-k]$$

This is nothing more than "collect every pair of indices whose sum is $n$" — written using a substitution $j = n-k$ so that as $k$ ranges over its values, $j$ automatically satisfies $k+j=n$. **This formula is the definition of convolution.** We just derived it purely from asking "what's the coefficient of $z^n$ when you multiply two polynomials" — no signals, no systems, just algebra.

## The critical reinterpretation: reflection + shifting

Here's the part that actually makes convolution _computable_ and _visualizable_, rather than just an abstract summation symbol — and it's the single most important intuition to nail before Module 6's hand-computation.

Look again at the term $b[n-k]$ inside the sum. Fix $n$ for a moment and think of this as a function of the _running variable_ $k$. Two things are happening to $b$ simultaneously:

1. **Reflection:** the appearance of $-k$ (rather than $+k$) means $b$ has been **flipped/reversed** — read backwards, right-to-left instead of left-to-right.
2. **Shift:** the appearance of $+n$ added on top means that reversed copy has then been **slid over** by $n$ positions.

So $b[n-k]$, viewed as a sequence indexed by $k$, literally _is_ "take $b$, flip it around $k=0$, then slide the flipped version so it's centered at position $n$." That's why the slides describe the whole procedure as: **"keep $a$ fixed, reflect $b$, then slide it across, and at each sliding position, multiply overlapping values and add them up."**

Let's watch this play out concretely with the same $a,b$ from before ($a = {1,3,2,5,4}$, $b={2,-1,4,1,3}$ at indices $0$–$4$).

**At $n=0$:** we need $b[0-k] = b[-k]$. This means comparing $a[k]$ against the _fully reflected_ $b$ (reflected around $k=0$), with no additional shift yet. Lining up index-by-index, only the position $k=0$ overlaps both sequences' "active" ranges in a way that gives a nonzero, single-term product here: $$c[0] = a[0],b[0] = (1)(2) = 2$$

**At $n=2$:** now we need $b[2-k]$ — the same reflected copy of $b$, but now _slid right by 2_. This changes which values of $a[k]$ and $b[2-k]$ line up and overlap: $$c[2] = a[0]b[2] + a[1]b[1] + a[2]b[0] = (1)(4)+(3)(-1)+(2)(2) = 4-3+4 = 5$$

**At $n=4$:** the reflected $b$ has now slid all the way so that _every_ term overlaps — this recovers exactly the same criss-cross sum we computed from the polynomial picture: $$c[4] = 17 \quad \checkmark$$

This confirms: **sliding the reflected sequence and reading off the overlap-sum at each position is _identical_ to collecting the criss-crossed polynomial coefficient pairs.** They're not two different methods that happen to agree — they're the _same calculation_, just described in two different but equivalent geometric languages (diagonal pairing vs. flip-and-slide).

## Why "reflect" specifically, and not something else

A question worth asking yourself: why does convolution reflect one of the sequences, rather than, say, just sliding it without reflecting? The answer is baked into the algebra: it's a direct, forced consequence of wanting $i+j=n$ to stay fixed. If instead you tried to line up $a[k]$ with $b[k]$ directly (no reflection, no sliding — like ordinary addition), you'd only ever capture pairs where the indices _match exactly_ ($i=j$), never pairs where $i+j$ sums to a _fixed target_ $n$. Reflection is what converts "indices that must sum to $n$" into "indices that must be vertically aligned after sliding" — it's a change of perspective that turns a diagonal-collection problem into a simple vertical-alignment problem, which is much easier to compute and visualize.

## Exercises — Module 5 (with answers)

**1.** Let $A(z) = 2 + z + 3z^2$ and $B(z) = 1 + 4z + 2z^2$. Using the criss-cross pairing method (not full expansion), compute the coefficient of $z^2$ in $A(z)B(z)$. Show every pair you used.

**2.** For the same $A(z), B(z)$ as in Question 1, what is the coefficient of $z^4$ in the product? (Think carefully about which pairs of indices are even _available_, given that both polynomials only go up to $z^2$.)

**3.** Using sequences $a = {a_0,a_1,a_2} = {2,1,3}$ and $b={b_0,b_1,b_2}={1,4,2}$ (same numbers as Q1, just relabeled as sequences instead of polynomial coefficients), compute $c[1] = (a*b)[1]$ using the **reflect-and-slide** interpretation directly (i.e., explicitly reflect $b$, shift it to position $n=1$, and multiply-and-add the overlap) — and confirm it matches what the criss-cross method would give for the coefficient of $z^1$.

**4. (Conceptual — the important one.)** Explain, in your own words, why the formula $c[n] = \sum_k a[k]b[n-k]$ automatically has $b$ appearing "reflected" rather than $a$. Then answer: if we had instead written the formula as $c[n]=\sum_k a[n-k]b[k]$ (reflecting $a$ instead of $b$), would we get a _different_ answer for $c[n]$, or the same one? Justify using the commutativity of ordinary multiplication in the original polynomial derivation (you don't need Module 7 yet — reason directly from the sum $\sum_k a[k]b[n-k]$ by substituting $k' = n-k$).

## Answers

**1.** We need all pairs $(i,j)$ with $i+j=2$, drawing from $a_0=2,a_1=1,a_2=3$ and $b_0=1,b_1=4,b_2=2$: $$c[2] = a_0b_2 + a_1b_1 + a_2b_0 = (2)(2) + (1)(4) + (3)(1) = 4+4+3 = 11$$

**2.** We'd need pairs $(i,j)$ with $i+j=4$, drawing only from available indices $i,j \in {0,1,2}$ (since both polynomials stop at $z^2$). The only pair within range is $(i,j)=(2,2)$ — e.g. $(i,j)=(3,1)$ or $(4,0)$ would require $a_3$ or $a_4$, which don't exist (they're implicitly zero). So: $$c[4] = a_2 b_2 = (3)(2) = 6$$ (Everything else in the "full" formula is multiplied by an implicit zero coefficient, so it drops out.)

**3.** We want $c[1] = \sum_k a[k],b[1-k]$.

First, reflect $b$: $b[-k]$ means reading $b$ backwards, so as a sequence indexed by $k$: $b[0]=1$ sits at $k=0$, $b[1]=4$ sits at $k=-1$, $b[2]=2$ sits at $k=-2$ (reflection flips the sign of the index).

Now shift this reflected sequence right by 1 (since we want $b[1-k]$, i.e., replace $k$ with $k-1$ in the reflected version): the value that was at $k=0$ moves to $k=1$, the value at $k=-1$ moves to $k=0$, the value at $k=-2$ moves to $k=-1$. So $b[1-k]$, as a function of $k$, equals: $1$ at $k=1$, $4$ at $k=0$, $2$ at $k=-1$.

Now multiply-and-add against $a[k]$ (which is $2$ at $k=0$, $1$ at $k=1$, $3$ at $k=2$), overlapping only where both are defined ($k=0$ and $k=1$): $$c[1] = a[0]\cdot b[1-0] + a[1]\cdot b[1-1] = a[0]b[1] + a[1]b[0] = (2)(4)+(1)(1) = 8+1 = 9$$

**Check against criss-cross:** coefficient of $z^1$ needs pairs with $i+j=1$: $(i,j) = (0,1)$ and $(1,0)$: $c[1] = a_0b_1+a_1b_0 = (2)(4)+(1)(1)=9$. ✓ Matches exactly.

**4.** The reflection on $b$ arises simply because of _which letter we chose to call the "running/dummy" index_ in the sum — we wrote the sum in terms of $k$ ranging over $a$'s natural index, and then $b$'s index is _forced_ to be $n-k$ to keep the total fixed at $n$ (since $k + (n-k) = n$ always). There's nothing fundamentally special that "picks" $b$ to be reflected — it's purely a labeling choice of which variable you're summing over.

If instead you wrote $c[n] = \sum_k a[n-k]b[k]$ (letting $k$ range over $b$'s index this time, forcing $a$'s index to be $n-k$), you get the **same answer**, not a different one. Proof: substitute $k' = n-k$ into the original sum $c[n]=\sum_k a[k]b[n-k]$. As $k$ ranges over all integers, $k'=n-k$ also ranges over all integers (just relabeled, and reversed in order, which doesn't matter for an infinite/full sum). Substituting $k = n-k'$ throughout: $$c[n] = \sum_k a[k]b[n-k] = \sum_{k'} a[n-k'],b[k']$$ which is _exactly_ $\sum_k a[n-k]b[k]$ after renaming the dummy variable $k'$ back to $k$ (dummy variable names never affect the value of a sum). So both formulas give identical results — this is really just a preview of the **commutativity of convolution** ($a_b = b_a$), which Module 7 will state formally, but you've now essentially proven it yourself directly from the summation definition, rather than just the polynomial-multiplication argument.

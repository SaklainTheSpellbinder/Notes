# Module 10: The Impulse Response

## The core idea, explained properly

Now that we know what an impulse _is_ (Module 9), we can finally define the single most important quantity in this entire lecture:

$$\delta[n] \xrightarrow{\text{LTI system } S} h[n]$$

**The impulse response $h[n]$ is simply whatever comes out when you poke the system with a single unit impulse.** That's the whole definition — no more, no less. But the reason this one signal deserves an entire module of attention is that, as Modules 4 and 8 already previewed, $h[n]$ is going to turn out to be **the complete, total, entire characterization of the system** — once you know $h[n]$, you know _everything_ the system will ever do to _any_ input, forever. So it's worth building real intuition for what different impulse responses _mean_ physically, before we get to the mechanical convolution machinery in Modules 11–13.

Let's walk through the three examples from the slides, extracting the actual physical meaning behind each — not just restating the formulas.

## Example 1: The "perfect echo chamber" — $h[n] = u[n]$

Picture the toy scenario from the slides: you say "hello" once (a single, brief sound — your impulse), and the room's walls bounce that sound back and forth, producing echoes. In this idealized toy system, suppose _every single echo comes back at exactly the same strength as the original_ — no fading, no loss, forever.

$$\delta[n] \longrightarrow h[n] = u[n]$$

Let's actually unpack why $u[n]$ (the unit step: $0$ before $n=0$, then $1$ forever after) represents "equal-strength echoes forever." Think about what $u[n]$ _looks like_: it's silent (zero) before the poke happens, and then, starting exactly when the poke occurs, it stays "on" at a constant value ($1$) for all time afterward, never decaying, never stopping.

Physically, this would model something like: you clap once, and the sound doesn't fade at all — it just keeps bouncing back at full strength, echo after echo after echo, forever. This is obviously _not_ physically realistic (real rooms absorb sound energy, so echoes fade) — but it's a useful **toy/idealized case** precisely because it's the simplest possible "non-trivial" impulse response: it's not _literally_ just "the poke passes straight through unchanged" (that would be $h[n]=\delta[n]$, meaning zero net effect from the system — the system just returns exactly what you gave it), but it's still simple enough to reason about by hand.

## Example 2: The realistic fading echo — $h[n] = \alpha^n u[n]$, $0<\alpha<1$

This is the physically sensible upgrade: real echoes _do_ fade, because each time sound bounces off a wall, some of its energy gets absorbed. So instead of every echo being equally strong forever, each successive echo is some fixed _fraction_ $\alpha$ as strong as the one before it:

$$h[n] = \alpha^n u[n] = {1, \alpha, \alpha^2, \alpha^3, \dots} \quad \text{for } n=0,1,2,3,\dots$$

**What does $\alpha$ actually mean, precisely?** It's the **fraction of echo strength that survives each successive reflection/time-step.** Let's make this completely concrete with actual numbers rather than just re-stating the slide's claim:

- If $\alpha = 0.9$: each echo retains $90%$ of the strength of the previous one. After 10 reflections, the surviving strength is $0.9^{10} \approx 0.35$ — still over a third of the original strength after 10 bounces. This is a "large," resonant, slowly-decaying room — think of a cathedral or a big empty hall, where echoes seem to linger.
    
- If $\alpha = 0.3$: each echo keeps only $30%$ of the previous one's strength. After just 3 reflections, the surviving strength is $0.3^3 = 0.027$ — down to about $2.7%$ of the original. This is a "dead," heavily damped room — think of a small room stuffed with thick carpets and curtains, where sound dies out almost immediately.
    

**Why is this exponential decay, and not, say, linear decay?** Because each echo's strength is a _fixed fraction_ of the _previous_ echo's strength (not a fixed fraction of the _original_). This "constant ratio between consecutive terms" is exactly the defining structure of a geometric sequence, and geometric sequences are exactly $\alpha^n$-shaped. This is a genuinely important pattern to recognize: **any time a physical process loses a constant proportion of "what's currently left" at each step (rather than losing a constant absolute amount each step), you get exponential decay, not linear decay.** This shows up everywhere beyond just acoustics — radioactive decay, capacitor discharge, cooling objects, population die-off — so this $\alpha^n u[n]$ impulse response is really a template for an enormous class of real physical systems, not just echoes.

**What happens at $\alpha=1$?** You recover Example 1 exactly ($1^n = 1$ for all $n$, so $h[n]=u[n]$) — the "no decay at all" limiting case. **What would $\alpha > 1$ mean?** The echoes would _grow_ stronger with each reflection — physically absurd for a passive echo (where would the extra energy come from?), but mathematically it corresponds to an _unstable_, runaway-amplifying system — a concept you'll likely meet formally later in the course under "BIBO stability," which is directly determined by properties of $h[n]$.

## Example 3: The pure delay — $h[n] = \delta[n-3]$

This is the "opposite extreme" from the echo examples — a system that does essentially _nothing_ except shift time.

$$y[n] = x[n-3]$$

If you poke this system with $\delta[n]$ (an impulse at $n=0$), by definition of the system it simply outputs a shifted copy: $\delta[n-3]$ (the exact same "spike," just relocated to $n=3$). So:

$$h[n] = \delta[n-3]$$

**Physical meaning:** this system introduces _no distortion whatsoever_ — no fading, no echoes, no reshaping — it just holds onto whatever you feed it for exactly 3 time-steps, then releases it unchanged. Think of a long cable or a fixed transmission delay: whatever signal goes in one end comes out the other end _identical_ in every respect, just later. This is the simplest possible _nontrivial_ impulse response you could have (the truly trivial case being $h[n]=\delta[n]$ itself, i.e. $y[n]=x[n]$, a system that does literally nothing at all — not even a delay).

**Continuous-time version:** $h(t) = \delta(t-3)$ — same idea, just using the continuous-time impulse from Module 9's Dirac-delta discussion.

## Tying these three examples together: reading the "shape" of $h[n]$ like a story

Here's the meta-skill this module is really trying to build: once you accept that $h[n]$ is "the system's signature," you should be able to **look at a given impulse response and immediately narrate, in plain language, what the system physically does** — this is a skill examiners love to test, because it requires genuine understanding rather than memorized formulas.

|Impulse response shape|What it tells you about the system|
|---|---|
|$h[n] = \delta[n]$|Does nothing at all — output equals input exactly|
|$h[n] = \delta[n-k]$|Pure delay by $k$ steps, no other distortion|
|$h[n] = a,\delta[n]$|Pure amplification/attenuation by factor $a$, no delay, no memory|
|$h[n] = u[n]$|Infinite, undamped memory — every past input contributes forever, undiminished|
|$h[n] = \alpha^n u[n]$, $0<\alpha<1$|Fading memory — recent inputs matter most, influence exponentially dies out|
|$h[n]$ nonzero only over a short finite range (e.g., $n=0,1,2$)|Short-term averaging/smoothing — only a _few_ recent input samples affect the current output, nothing before that|

This last row is worth flagging since it connects to your Python lab: something like $h[n] = {1/3, 1/3, 1/3}$ (nonzero only at $n=0,1,2$) is precisely a **moving-average smoothing filter** — a classic, extremely common real-world impulse response you'll likely code up and convolve against test signals.

## Exercises — Module 10 (with answers)

**1.** A system has impulse response $h[n] = 2\delta[n] - \delta[n-1]$. In plain English (no formulas), describe what this system physically does to an arbitrary input signal — specifically, describe the relationship between the current output sample and the input samples it depends on.

**2.** Two rooms are tested with a hand clap (an impulse). Room A gives impulse response $h_A[n] = (0.8)^n u[n]$; Room B gives $h_B[n] = (0.2)^n u[n]$. Without computing any numbers, explain which room sounds "livelier" (more reverberant/echoey) and which sounds "deader," and justify your answer using the physical meaning of $\alpha$ established above.

**3.** Compute the numeric value of $h_A[n]$ (from Question 2) at $n=0,1,2,3,4,5$, and separately compute $h_B[n]$ at the same points. At what value of $n$ does Room B's echo strength first drop below $1%$ of its original strength? (Show the calculation, don't just estimate.)

**4. (Conceptual — connects to Module 4/9.)** Explain why the impulse response of the "does nothing" system must be $h[n]=\delta[n]$ and _not_ $h[n]=0$ for all $n$ (i.e., not the all-zero sequence). Frame your answer around what "the system does nothing" _means_ as applied specifically to the _one specific input_ $\delta[n]$ being fed in.

## Answers

**1.** This system produces, at each time $n$, an output equal to **twice the current input sample minus the input sample from one step earlier**: $y[n] = 2x[n] - x[n-1]$ (this follows directly from convolving $x[n]$ with $h[n]$, though you're not required to derive that yet — Module 12 will formalize why $h[n]$ directly tells you this). In plain terms: the system amplifies the "instantaneous" part of the signal (doubling the current sample) while simultaneously _subtracting off_ a delayed copy of the signal — this is a simple example of an **edge-detector/difference-emphasizing filter**: it responds strongly when the signal is _changing_ from one sample to the next (since it computes something like "twice now, minus what it was a moment ago"), and responds weakly (or cancels out) when the signal is holding roughly steady.

**2.** Room A ($\alpha=0.8$) sounds **livelier/more reverberant** — since $\alpha$ represents the _fraction of echo strength retained per step_, a larger $\alpha$ (0.8, close to 1) means each successive echo only loses a _small_ fraction of its strength, so echoes persist for a long time before fading into inaudibility — like a big cathedral or empty gymnasium.

Room B ($\alpha=0.2$) sounds **deader/more damped** — a smaller $\alpha$ (0.2, close to 0) means each echo loses the _majority_ of its strength every single step, so the sound dies out almost immediately after the original clap — like a small room full of sound-absorbing furniture and carpets.

**3.** $h_A[n] = 0.8^n$: $n=0: 1$, $n=1: 0.8$, $n=2: 0.64$, $n=3: 0.512$, $n=4: 0.4096$, $n=5: 0.32768$

$h_B[n] = 0.2^n$: $n=0: 1$, $n=1: 0.2$, $n=2: 0.04$, $n=3: 0.008$, $n=4: 0.0016$, $n=5: 0.00032$

For Room B, we want the smallest $n$ such that $0.2^n < 0.01$:

- $n=2$: $0.04$ — still above $1%$
- $n=3$: $0.008$ — this is below $0.01$ ✓

**Room B first drops below 1% strength at $n=3$** (three time-steps after the original clap) — confirming numerically just how fast the "dead room" example decays compared to how slowly Room A would (Room A wouldn't cross below 1% until around $n=21$, since $0.8^{21}\approx 0.0092$ — dramatically longer, exactly matching the "lively vs. dead" intuition from Question 2).

**4.** "The system does nothing" means, precisely: **whatever signal you feed in, that exact same signal comes back out, completely unchanged** — i.e., $y[n] = x[n]$ for every possible input $x[n]$, including the specific case $x[n]=\delta[n]$.

If we feed in $x[n]=\delta[n]$ specifically, and the system truly "does nothing," then by definition $y[n]$ must equal $x[n]$ exactly — meaning $y[n] = \delta[n]$, **not** $y[n]=0$. The confusion of "does nothing" with "outputs zero" is a subtle trap: "outputs zero" would actually describe a _different_, much more drastic system — one that destroys _all_ information and outputs silence no matter what you feed it (a system with $h[n]=0$ for all $n$ would, via the convolution formula from Module 12, produce $y[n]=0$ for literally _every_ input, not just for the impulse — a totally "dead," useless system that discards all information). "Doing nothing" (the identity system) and "destroying everything" (the zero system) are polar opposites, and the only way to distinguish them via their impulse response is to remember that $h[n]$ is _itself_ defined as "the output when the _specific_ input $\delta[n]$ is used" — so $h[n]=\delta[n]$ correctly encodes "input passed through unchanged," while $h[n]=0$ would (correctly) encode "everything gets erased, always."

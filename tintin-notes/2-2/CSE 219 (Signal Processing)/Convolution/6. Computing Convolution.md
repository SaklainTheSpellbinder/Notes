# Module 6: Computing Convolution Step-by-Step

## The three-step recipe, stated cleanly

From Module 5, we know: $$y[n] = (x*h)[n] = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} x[k],h[n-k]$$

To compute this by hand, every single time, you do exactly three mechanical things:

1. **Reflect** one sequence (say $h[k] \to h[-k]$) — flip it left-to-right around $k=0$.
2. **Shift** the reflected sequence to position $n$ (i.e., $h[-k] \to h[n-k]$) — slide it right by $n$ (or left, if $n$ is negative).
3. **Multiply and add** — wherever $x[k]$ and the shifted-reflected $h[n-k]$ both have nonzero values at the _same_ $k$, multiply those two numbers together, then sum all such products. That sum is $y[n]$.

You repeat this whole three-step dance **once per value of $n$** — a fresh reflect-shift-multiply-add for $n=0$, then again for $n=1$, then $n=2$, and so on, until the sequences no longer overlap at all (output becomes zero).

## Why the output gets _longer_ than either input

This is worth flagging before diving into arithmetic, because it surprises people: if $x[n]$ has 4 samples (say, indices 0–3) and $h[n]$ has 4 samples (indices 0–3), the output $y[n]$ will have **7** samples (indices 0–6), not 4. General rule: convolving a length-$N$ sequence with a length-$M$ sequence gives a length-$(N+M-1)$ result. Intuition: think of the sliding picture — the reflected sequence starts by _barely touching_ the other sequence at one edge (tiny overlap, giving small/simple output values near the start), grows to _maximum overlap_ somewhere in the middle (biggest, richest output values), then shrinks back down to _barely touching_ at the other edge before disappearing completely (small/simple output values near the end). Every sliding position where there's _any_ overlap at all contributes one output sample — and that range of positions is longer than either original sequence.

## Full worked example, reproduced with complete reasoning at each step

Let's use exactly the example from your slides: $$x[n]: \quad x[0]=1,\ x[1]=2,\ x[2]=1,\ x[3]=1 \qquad (\text{zero elsewhere})$$ $$h[n]: \quad h[0]=1,\ h[1]=1,\ h[2]=-1,\ h[3]=2 \qquad (\text{zero elsewhere})$$

**Step 0 — reflect $h$:** Flip $h[k]$ around $k=0$ to get $h[-k]$. The value that was at $k=0$ stays at $k=0$; the value that was at $k=1$ moves to $k=-1$; at $k=2$ moves to $k=-2$; at $k=3$ moves to $k=-3$. So as a function of $k$: $$h[-k]: \quad h[-(-3)]=h[3]=2 \text{ at } k=-3,\quad h[1]=1 \text{ at } k=-1,\quad h[2]=-1 \text{ at }k=-2, \quad h[0]=1 \text{ at } k=0$$ i.e. reading left to right by $k$: at $k=-3,-2,-1,0$ the values are $2, -1, 1, 1$.

**Now we slide this reflected copy rightward, one step at a time, and at each stop, multiply-and-add against the fixed $x[k]$.**

### $n=0$: slide amount = 0

The reflected $h$ sits at $k=-3,-2,-1,0$ with values $2,-1,1,1$. Compare against $x[k]$, which lives at $k=0,1,2,3$ with values $1,2,1,1$. **Overlap** — the only $k$ where _both_ sequences are simultaneously nonzero — is just $k=0$. $$y[0] = x[0]\cdot h[0-0] = x[0]\cdot h[0] = (1)(1) = 1$$

### $n=1$: slide reflected $h$ right by 1 more step

Now the reflected-and-shifted $h[1-k]$ lives at $k=-2,-1,0,1$ with the same values $2,-1,1,1$ (just moved one step right). Overlap with $x[k]$ (living at $k=0,1,2,3$) now happens at $k=0$ and $k=1$. $$y[1] = x[0]h[1-0] + x[1]h[1-1] = x[0]h[1] + x[1]h[0] = (1)(1)+(2)(1) = 1+2 = 3$$

### $n=2$: overlap grows to three terms

$h[2-k]$ now lives at $k=-1,0,1,2$. Overlap with $x[k]$ at $k=0,1,2,3$ happens at $k=0,1,2$. $$y[2] = x[0]h[2] + x[1]h[1] + x[2]h[0] = (1)(-1)+(2)(1)+(1)(1) = -1+2+1 = 2$$

### $n=3$: full overlap — all four terms present

$h[3-k]$ lives at $k=0,1,2,3$ — now it lines up _exactly_ with $x[k]$'s support. This is the maximum-overlap position. $$y[3] = x[0]h[3]+x[1]h[2]+x[2]h[1]+x[3]h[0] = (1)(2)+(2)(-1)+(1)(1)+(1)(1) = 2-2+1+1 = 2$$

### $n=4$: overlap starts shrinking from the _left_ edge

$h[4-k]$ lives at $k=1,2,3,4$. Now the left edge ($k=0$ of $x$) no longer has a matching $h$-value in range; overlap is at $k=1,2,3$. $$y[4] = x[1]h[3]+x[2]h[2]+x[3]h[1] = (2)(2)+(1)(-1)+(1)(1) = 4-1+1 = 4$$

### $n=5$: overlap down to two terms

$h[5-k]$ lives at $k=2,3,4,5$. Overlap with $x[k]$'s support ($k=0..3$) is only $k=2,3$. $$y[5] = x[2]h[3]+x[3]h[2] = (1)(2)+(1)(-1) = 2-1 = 1$$

### $n=6$: down to one term — the last nonzero output

$h[6-k]$ lives at $k=3,4,5,6$. Overlap is just $k=3$. $$y[6] = x[3]h[3] = (1)(2) = 2$$

### $n=7$ and beyond: zero

The reflected-shifted $h$ has moved entirely past $x$'s support — no overlap, output is zero forever after.

## Final result

$$y[n] = {1,\ 3,\ 2,\ 2,\ 4,\ 1,\ 2} \quad \text{for } n=0,1,2,3,4,5,6$$

Notice the shape: it doesn't monotonically grow then shrink perfectly symmetrically (that only happens if both sequences are "nice" like rectangular pulses) — the actual values wiggle because $h$ has a negative entry ($h[2]=-1$) mixed in, which is exactly why $y[2]$ and $y[3]$ come out smaller than $y[4]$ even though the overlap is "more complete" at $n=3$. **The length pattern (grow-then-shrink) is guaranteed by the number of overlapping terms; the actual values depend entirely on the specific numbers being multiplied.**

## A practical shortcut for hand computation (useful for your exam)

Instead of mentally reflecting-and-sliding every time, many people find it faster to use this equivalent mechanical rule, sometimes called the "multiply-shift-add" table method:

For each $n$, $y[n] = \sum_k x[k] h[n-k]$ — so just ask: **"which pairs of indices $(k, n-k)$, both within the valid range of $x$ and $h$ respectively, exist for this $n$?"** Then sum $x[k]\cdot h[n-k]$ over exactly those pairs. This is identical to the criss-cross diagonal-pairing idea from Module 5 — you're just now doing it directly with the sequences instead of with polynomial coefficients. For example, at $n=4$ with $x$ having valid indices ${0,1,2,3}$ and $h$ having valid indices ${0,1,2,3}$: we need $k \in {0,1,2,3}$ **and** $4-k \in {0,1,2,3}$ simultaneously → this forces $k \in {1,2,3}$ (since $k=0$ gives $4-k=4$, out of range). That instantly matches what we found above without needing to draw the sliding picture at all — just index bookkeeping.

## Answers

**1.** $x$ has indices $0,1,2$ (length 3), $h$ has indices $0,1$ (length 2). Expected output length: $3+2-1=4$, indices $n=0,1,2,3$.

- $n=0$: only pair $(k,n-k)=(0,0)$ valid. $y[0]=x[0]h[0]=(2)(3)=6$
- $n=1$: pairs $(0,1)$ and $(1,0)$ valid. $y[1]=x[0]h[1]+x[1]h[0] = (2)(1)+(0)(3) = 2+0=2$
- $n=2$: pairs $(1,1)$ and $(2,0)$ valid ($k=0,n-k=2$ invalid since $h[2]$ doesn't exist). $y[2]=x[1]h[1]+x[2]h[0]=(0)(1)+(-1)(3)=0-3=-3$
- $n=3$: only pair $(2,1)$ valid. $y[3]=x[2]h[1]=(-1)(1)=-1$

Full output: $y[n] = {6, 2, -3, -1}$ for $n=0,1,2,3$ — length 4. ✓ Matches $3+2-1=4$.

**2.** For $n=1$: we need $k$ such that $x[k]$ is valid (i.e. $k\in{0,1,2}$) **and** $h[1-k]$ is valid (i.e. $1-k \in {0,1}$, meaning $k \in {0,1}$). Intersection: $k\in{0,1}$. So: $$y[1] = x[0]h[1] + x[1]h[0] = (2)(1)+(0)(3) = 2$$ Matches Q1's $y[1]=2$. ✓

**3.** $x$ starts at $n=-1$ (3 samples: $-1,0,1$), $h$ starts at $n=0$ (2 samples: $0,1$). Expected output length $3+2-1=4$; expected starting index of output = $(-1)+0 = -1$, so output should span $n=-1,0,1,2$.

- $n=-1$: need $k\in{-1,0,1}$ and $-1-k \in {0,1}$, i.e. $k \in {-2,-1}$. Intersection: $k=-1$. $y[-1]=x[-1]h[-1-(-1)]=x[-1]h[0]=(1)(1)=1$
- $n=0$: need $k\in{-1,0,1}$ and $-k\in{0,1}$, i.e. $k\in{-1,0}$. Intersection: $k\in{-1,0}$. $y[0]=x[-1]h[1]+x[0]h[0] = (1)(1)+(2)(1)=1+2=3$
- $n=1$: need $k\in{-1,0,1}$ and $1-k\in{0,1}$, i.e. $k\in{0,1}$. Intersection: $k\in{0,1}$. $y[1]=x[0]h[1]+x[1]h[0]=(2)(1)+(-1)(1)=2-1=1$
- $n=2$: need $k\in{-1,0,1}$ and $2-k\in{0,1}$, i.e. $k\in{1,2}$. Intersection: $k=1$. $y[2]=x[1]h[1]=(-1)(1)=-1$

Full output: $y[n]={1,3,1,-1}$ for $n=-1,0,1,2$ — confirms the starting-index rule (sum of starting indices) and the length rule (4 samples). ✓

**4.** The issue is that $x[3]h[-1]$ is **not a legitimate term of the sum at all** — $h[-1]$ isn't "zero because it's missing from the list," it's zero _by definition_ because $h[n]$ is only defined to be nonzero on $n=0,1,2,3$, meaning $h[-1]=0$ is a _given fact about the signal_, not a convenient assumption. The student's method — write out all four cross-terms mechanically ($x[0..3]$ against $h[2],h[1],h[0],h[-1]$) and then patch up out-of-range terms by declaring them zero after the fact — works _by luck_ here, but it's fragile and dangerous as a general method for two reasons: (1) it's easy to forget to zero out an invalid term, especially with longer sequences where several terms might be out of range simultaneously, and (2) it obscures _why_ the sum has the particular number of terms it has, making it much harder to catch an error like an off-by-one in the index range. The **stricter approach** — determine the valid intersection of indices _first_ (as done systematically in the worked example and in Q1–Q3 above) — guarantees you only ever write down terms that are mathematically guaranteed to be nonzero-eligible, so there's no reliance on getting lucky with a stray zero; it also directly tells you _how many terms_ to expect before you even start multiplying, which is a strong self-check against arithmetic slips.

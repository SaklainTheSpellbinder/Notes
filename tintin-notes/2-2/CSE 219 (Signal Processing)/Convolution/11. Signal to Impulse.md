# Module 11: Decomposing Any Signal into Impulses

## The core idea, explained properly

This is **Step 2** from the Module 8 roadmap — the signals-world counterpart of "every integer has a unique prime factorization." We need to show that _any_ discrete-time signal $x[n]$, no matter how wild or complicated, can always be rebuilt exactly from scaled, shifted copies of the humble impulse $\delta[n]$.

The claim, stated formally:

$$x[n] = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} x[k],\delta[n-k]$$

At first glance this looks almost circular — "$x[n]$ equals a sum involving $x[k]$"? — so let's slow down and make sure the logic is airtight, because this formula is going to be the entire foundation for Modules 12–13.

## Building the intuition from scratch: one sample at a time

Forget the general formula for a moment. Ask a much more concrete question: **how would you build the single number $x[0]$ using only impulses?**

Well, $\delta[n]$ is $1$ exactly at $n=0$ and $0$ everywhere else. So if you take $x[0]\cdot\delta[n]$ — the impulse _scaled_ by the constant $x[0]$ — you get a signal that equals $x[0]$ at $n=0$, and $0$ everywhere else. That's a signal that matches $x[n]$ perfectly at $n=0$ (both are $x[0]$ there) — but is silent everywhere else, which is fine, because we haven't tried to account for the other samples of $x[n]$ yet.

Now: how do you build a signal that captures _just_ the value $x[1]$, contributing at $n=1$ specifically, and nowhere else? You need an impulse that "lives" at $n=1$ instead of $n=0$ — that's $\delta[n-1]$ (shifted impulse, Module 9, Property 1). Scale it by $x[1]$: $x[1]\cdot\delta[n-1]$ is a signal equal to $x[1]$ at $n=1$, and $0$ everywhere else.

Do this for _every_ index $k$: $x[k]\cdot\delta[n-k]$ is a signal that is silent everywhere, **except** at $n=k$, where it equals exactly $x[k]$.

**Now stack all of these up — add every one of these single-point contributions together, one for every integer $k$:**

$$\sum_k x[k],\delta[n-k]$$

Let's check this really does reconstruct $x[n]$ at some _specific_ index, say $n=5$. In the sum, every single term $x[k]\delta[5-k]$ is zero **except** the one term where $k=5$ (since $\delta[5-k]$ is only nonzero when $5-k=0$, i.e. $k=5$). That one surviving term contributes exactly $x[5]\cdot\delta[0] = x[5]\cdot 1 = x[5]$. Every other term vanishes because its impulse isn't "activated" at $n=5$.

So the sum, evaluated at $n=5$, gives exactly $x[5]$ — the correct value. And this argument works _identically_ for any $n$ you pick, not just $n=5$ — at every index $n$, exactly one term in the infinite sum "wakes up" (the $k=n$ term), and it contributes precisely $x[n]$, while every other term stays silent. That's the entire proof, and it's really just the **sifting property** from Module 9 (Property 2) applied inside a summation instead of to a single term.

## Why this decomposition is _guaranteed_ to work for literally any signal

This is worth stating explicitly, because it's exactly the "no exceptions" guarantee that Module 8's analogy demanded (recall: "just as every integer is guaranteed a prime factorization, every signal is guaranteed an impulse decomposition"). Notice the derivation above never assumed anything special about $x[n]$ — it didn't need $x[n]$ to be smooth, periodic, finite-length, positive, or anything else. The argument only used the sifting property of $\delta[n-k]$, which holds unconditionally. So **every discrete-time signal whatsoever admits this decomposition** — there's no signal "too weird" to be built this way.

## Why the decomposition is also _unique_ (this closes the loop from Module 8, Exercise 3)

Recall from Module 8's exercises: we flagged that _uniqueness_, not just existence, of the decomposition matters — otherwise "combine via convolution" in Step 3 would be ambiguous. Here's why uniqueness holds: the formula $x[n]=\sum_k x[k]\delta[n-k]$ doesn't leave you any freedom to _choose_ different coefficients — the coefficient multiplying $\delta[n-k]$ is **forced** to be exactly $x[k]$, the signal's own actual value at that index. There's no alternative set of coefficients $c[k] \ne x[k]$ that could also reconstruct $x[n]$ correctly, because if you evaluate any candidate decomposition $\sum_k c[k]\delta[n-k]$ at $n = k_0$ for some specific $k_0$, sifting forces the result to equal $c[k_0]$ exactly — and for this to match the true signal value $x[k_0]$, you need $c[k_0] = x[k_0]$. Since this holds for every $k_0$, the coefficients are pinned down uniquely; there's only one valid decomposition.

## Visualizing it: reading off a signal as a "staircase of pokes"

Picture a signal $x[n]$ plotted as a bar graph (as is standard for discrete-time signals) — a bunch of vertical spikes at different heights across different integer positions. The impulse decomposition is just the statement: **"this whole picture is nothing more than a bunch of individual, independent spikes, each one being its own scaled-and-shifted impulse, all just superimposed on the same axis."** You're not doing anything clever or transformative to the signal — you're just relabeling the _already-existing_ structure of a discrete sequence (a bunch of isolated sample values at isolated integer points) as _literally being_ a sum of scaled impulses. In discrete time, this is almost more of an _observation_ than a "derivation" — every discrete sequence, by its very nature as a list of values at isolated points, _already looks like_ a sum of scaled impulses; the formula just makes this explicit and gives you the tool to manipulate it algebraically.

## Why we bothered proving something that seems "obvious"

It's worth pausing on why this modest-looking fact deserves its own module. The _decomposition itself_ is nearly trivial once you see it (as the "staircase of pokes" picture shows). But its **payoff** is enormous, and it's entirely about what comes next: because you can write _any_ $x[n]$ this way, and because you know (from Module 10) exactly what an LTI system does to _each individual piece_ $x[k]\delta[n-k]$ — Module 12 is going to show that linearity and time-invariance let you determine the system's response to the _entire sum_ just by adding up the known responses to each piece. The hard work of "Step 2" isn't the algebra of the decomposition — it's establishing, rigorously, that this decomposition is universal and unambiguous, so that the machinery in Module 12 is guaranteed to apply to _every_ possible input, with nothing left to chance.

**2.** Verify your answer to Question 1 is correct by evaluating your written-out sum at $n=-1$, $n=0$, $n=1$, and $n=2$ separately, showing that only one term survives at each evaluation point (state explicitly which term survives, and why the others vanish).

**3.** A classmate claims: _"The decomposition formula $x[n]=\sum_k x[k]\delta[n-k]$ only works for signals that are eventually zero (finite-length signals) — for a signal like $x[n]=n$ (nonzero forever, growing without bound), the decomposition must break down since the sum has infinitely many nonzero terms."_ Is this claim correct? Explain precisely why or why not, referring back to how the sifting argument works at any _single, fixed_ value of $n$ (regardless of how many terms are "in play" globally).

**4. (Conceptual — the important one, directly testing uniqueness.)** Suppose someone proposes an _alternative_ decomposition for a signal $x[n]$, using **doubled** coefficients paired with **half-height** impulses: $x[n] \stackrel{?}{=} \sum_k \big(2x[k]\big)\cdot\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\delta[n-k]\right)$.

(a) Show this expression is _numerically_ equal to the standard decomposition (i.e., it still correctly reconstructs $x[n]$).

(b) Given this, does this contradict the "uniqueness" claim made in this module (that the coefficients multiplying $\delta[n-k]$ are uniquely forced to be $x[k]$)? ## Answers

**1.** $$x[n] = 2,\delta[n-(-1)] + (-3),\delta[n-0] + 5,\delta[n-1] = 2,\delta[n+1] - 3,\delta[n] + 5,\delta[n-1]$$

**2.**

- At $n=-1$: term 1: $2\delta[-1+1]=2\delta[0]=2$. Term 2: $-3\delta[-1]=-3(0)=0$ (since $-1\ne 0$). Term 3: $5\delta[-1-1]=5\delta[-2]=5(0)=0$ (since $-2\ne0$). Sum $=2+0+0=2$. Matches $x[-1]=2$. ✓ (Only the first term survives, since only its impulse is "activated" at $n=-1$.)
    
- At $n=0$: term 1: $2\delta[0+1]=2\delta[1]=0$. Term 2: $-3\delta[0]=-3(1)=-3$. Term 3: $5\delta[0-1]=5\delta[-1]=0$. Sum $=0-3+0=-3$. Matches $x[0]=-3$. ✓ (Only the second term survives.)
    
- At $n=1$: term 1: $2\delta[1+1]=2\delta[2]=0$. Term 2: $-3\delta[1]=0$. Term 3: $5\delta[1-1]=5\delta[0]=5$. Sum $=0+0+5=5$. Matches $x[1]=5$. ✓ (Only the third term survives.)
    
- At $n=2$: term 1: $2\delta[3]=0$. Term 2: $-3\delta[2]=0$. Term 3: $5\delta[1]=0$. Sum $=0$. Correctly matches $x[2]=0$ (which is indeed what the original signal specifies, since it's zero outside $n=-1,0,1$). ✓ (**No** term survives here — correctly signaling that $n=2$ is outside the signal's support.)
    

**3.** The claim is **incorrect**. The sifting argument (Module 11's core proof) never actually requires you to examine "all terms simultaneously" or worry about how many terms are globally nonzero — it works by fixing **one specific value of $n$ at a time**, and observing that at that fixed $n$, only the _single_ term with $k=n$ survives (everything else is forced to zero by the impulse's defining property), **regardless of how many total terms the full sum contains or whether the signal ever becomes zero.**

For $x[n]=n$: at any specific evaluation point, say $n=1000$, the sum $\sum_k k,\delta[1000-k]$ still only has one surviving term — the $k=1000$ term, contributing exactly $1000\cdot\delta[0]=1000$ — matching $x[1000]=1000$ perfectly, exactly the same way it worked for the finite examples in Questions 1–2. The fact that infinitely many terms exist _across all possible values of $n$_ is irrelevant, because we only ever need to evaluate the sum **at one point at a time** — and at any single point, it's always effectively a "one nonzero term" situation, whether the underlying signal is finite-length, infinite-length, bounded, or unbounded. The decomposition works universally, exactly as claimed in the "no exceptions" guarantee.

**4.** (a) Simplify the proposed alternative: $(2x[k])\cdot(\tfrac{1}{2}\delta[n-k]) = 2\cdot\tfrac12\cdot x[k]\delta[n-k] = x[k]\delta[n-k]$ — this is **algebraically identical**, term by term, to the original decomposition. So yes, it trivially reconstructs $x[n]$ correctly too, since it's not actually a different sum at all — it's the exact same sum, just with each term's constant factors split apart and regrouped differently ($2\times\frac12=1$, contributing nothing new).

(b) This does **not** contradict uniqueness, because uniqueness refers specifically to the coefficient multiplying the _standard, unscaled_ impulse $\delta[n-k]$ — i.e., once you insist on writing the decomposition in the canonical form $\sum_k c[k],\delta[n-k]$ (a single coefficient times a single, standard unit impulse at each shift), that coefficient $c[k]$ is uniquely forced to equal $x[k]$, with no freedom to choose otherwise. The "alternative" proposal in this question isn't actually a genuinely different decomposition in that canonical sense — it's the _same_ decomposition, merely with the constant "$1$" multiplying $\delta[n-k]$ artificially split into "$2 \times \frac12$" and cosmetically distributed between the two factors. If you multiply it back out (as in part (a)), you get back the identical, single coefficient $x[k]$ on the identical, single standard impulse $\delta[n-k]$ — so there's no real ambiguity here, just a superficial rewriting. True non-uniqueness would require finding _genuinely different_ values $c[k] \ne x[k]$ that _still_ correctly reconstruct $x[n]$ when paired with the _standard_ (unscaled) impulse $\delta[n-k]$ — and the sifting argument in this module proves that's impossible.

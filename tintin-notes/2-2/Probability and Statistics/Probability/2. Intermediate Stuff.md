# 1. Odds

  

Your Lecture 3 begins by defining odds as a comparison between an event happening and not happening.

  

This is different from probability.

  

Suppose:

  

$$

P(A)=\frac34

$$

  

This means:

  

> Out of the whole probability mass, $\frac34$ belongs to A.

  

But odds compare:

  

$$

A:\bar A

$$

  

Since

  

$$

P(\bar A)=1-\frac34=\frac14

$$

  

the odds in favor of A are:

  

$$

\frac{3/4}{1/4}=3

$$

  

or

  

$$

\boxed{3:1}

$$

  

Interpretation:

  

> For every 1 "part" against A, there are 3 parts in favor of A.

  

---

  

# 2. Probability vs Odds

  

This distinction is important.

  

Suppose 10 equally likely outcomes exist and A occurs in 7.

  

Probability:

  

$$

P(A)=\frac7{10}

$$

  

Odds in favor:

  

$$

7:3

$$

  

because:

  

- 7 favorable

- 3 unfavorable.

  

So:

  

$$

\boxed{\text{Probability}= \frac{\text{favorable}}{\text{total}}}

$$

  

while

  

$$

\boxed{\text{Odds in favor}= \frac{\text{favorable}}{\text{unfavorable}}}

$$

  

Do not confuse their denominators.

  

---

  

# 3. Odds in Favor

  

Your slide gives:

  

$$

\boxed{\text{Odds in favor of A}=\frac{P(A)}{P(\bar A)}}

$$

  

or, for equally likely outcomes,

  

$$

\boxed{ \frac{n(A)}{n(\bar A)} }

$$

  
  
  

If:

  

$$

P(A)=p

$$

  

then:

  

$$

P(\bar A)=1-p

$$

  

so:

  

$$

\boxed{ O_A=\frac{p}{1-p} }

$$

  

---

  

# 4. Odds Against

  

Odds against simply reverse the comparison:

  

$$

\boxed{\text{Odds against A}=\frac{P(\bar A)}{P(A)}}

$$

  

Your lecture points out that odds in favor and odds against are reciprocals.

  

Example:

  

$$

P(A)=0.2

$$

  

Then:

  

$$

P(\bar A)=0.8

$$

  

Odds in favor:

  

$$

0.2:0.8=1:4

$$

  

Odds against:

  

$$

0.8:0.2=4:1

$$

  

So:

  

$$

\boxed{\text{odds in favor}=1:4}

$$

  

$$

\boxed{\text{odds against}=4:1}

$$

  

---

  

# 5. Converting Odds → Probability

  

This should become automatic.

  

Suppose odds **in favor** of A are:

  

$$

a:b

$$

  

That means:

  

- favorable parts = (a)

- unfavorable parts = (b)

  

total parts:

  

$$

a+b

$$

  

Therefore:

  

$$

\boxed{P(A)=\frac{a}{a+b}}

$$

  

and:

  

$$

\boxed{P(\bar A)=\frac{b}{a+b}}

$$

  

Your slides give exactly this conversion.

  

---

  

## Example

  

Odds in favor are:

  

$$

5:3

$$

  

Then:

  

$$

P(A)=\frac5{5+3}=\boxed{\frac58}

$$

  

---

  

# 6. Odds Against → Probability

  

This is where students often reverse the numbers.

  

Suppose:

  

> Odds **against** A are $8:6$.

  

This means:

  

$$

\bar A:A=8:6

$$

  

Therefore:

  

$$

P(A)=\frac6{8+6} =\frac6{14} =\boxed{\frac37}

$$

  

and:

  

$$

P(\bar A)=\frac8{14} =\boxed{\frac47}

$$

  

Do **not** write $P(A)=8/14$, because the first number belongs to failure when the wording says "odds against".

  

---

  

# Quick translation rule

  

| Wording | Ratio |

|---|---|

| odds in favor of A = $a:b$ | $A:\bar A=a:b$ |

| odds against A = $a:b$ | $\bar A:A=a:b$ |

  

This is probably the safest way to remember it.

Suppose:

Then:

  

$$

P(\bar A)=\frac58

$$

  

Odds in favor:

  

$$

\frac38:\frac58

$$

  

Multiply both by 8:

\boxed{3:5}

Odds against:

# 9. "Both"

  

If X and Y both happen:

  

$$

\boxed{X\cap Y}

$$

  

If independent:

  

$$

\boxed{P(X\cap Y)=P(X)P(Y)}

$$

  

If dependent:

  

$$

\boxed{P(X\cap Y)=P(X)P(Y\mid X)}

$$

  

We will distinguish these carefully.

  

---

  

# 10. "Neither"

  

Neither X nor Y means:

  

$$

\bar X\cap\bar Y

$$

  

Using De Morgan:

  

$$

\bar X\cap\bar Y=\overline{X\cup Y}

$$

  

Therefore:

  

$$

\boxed{P(\text{neither})=1-P(X\cup Y)}

$$

  

This complement approach is extremely useful.

  

---

  

# 11. "At Least One"

  

At least one of X and Y means:

  

- X alone

- Y alone

- both.

  

Therefore:

  

$$

\boxed{X\cup Y}

$$

  

and:

  

$$

\boxed{P(X\cup Y)=P(X)+P(Y)-P(X\cap Y)}

$$

  

Or, often easier:

  

$$

\boxed{P(\text{at least one})=1-P(\text{neither})}

$$

  

---

  

# 12. Why Do We Subtract the Intersection?

  

Suppose:

  

$$

P(X)=0.5,\qquad P(Y)=0.4

$$

  

and:

  

$$

P(X\cap Y)=0.2

$$

  

If you do:

  

$$

0.5+0.4=0.9

$$

  

the people/outcomes in both X and Y were counted twice.

  

So remove one copy:

  

$$

0.5+0.4-0.2=\boxed{0.7}

$$

  

Hence:

  

$$

\boxed{P(X\cup Y)=P(X)+P(Y)-P(X\cap Y)}

$$

  

---

  

# 13. "Exactly One"

  

Exactly one means:

  

- X happens, Y doesn't

  

OR

  

- Y happens, X doesn't.

  

Therefore:

  

$$

\boxed{P(\text{exactly one})=P(X\cap\bar Y)+P(\bar X\cap Y)}

$$

  

There is also a very useful shortcut:

  

$$

X\cup Y

$$

  

contains:

  

- X only

- Y only

- both.

  

If you remove "both", exactly one remains.

  

Therefore:

  

$$

\boxed{P(\text{exactly one})=P(X\cup Y)-P(X\cap Y)}

$$

  

Your slides use both approaches.

  

---

  

# 14. "Either X or Y"

  

Your lecture treats:

  

> either X or Y

  

as **inclusive OR**:

  

$$

X\cup Y

$$

  

meaning X, Y, **or both**.

  

But this wording can be ambiguous in ordinary English.

  

If a problem says:

  

> either X or Y, **but not both**

  

then it means exactly one.

  

So distinguish:

  

$$

\boxed{\text{inclusive OR}=X\cup Y}

$$

  

from:

  

$$

\boxed{\text{exclusive OR}=\text{exactly one}}

$$

# 20. A Very Useful Picture in Your Head

  

For two events X and Y, divide everything into four mutually exclusive regions:

  

$$

X\cap Y

$$

  

$$

X\cap\bar Y

$$

  

$$

\bar X\cap Y

$$

  

$$

\bar X\cap\bar Y

$$

  

These four pieces cover the entire sample space.

  

So:

  

$$

\boxed{ P(X\cap Y)+ P(X\cap\bar Y)+ P(\bar X\cap Y)+ P(\bar X\cap\bar Y) =1 }

$$

  

Almost every two-event problem is just asking you to combine some of these four regions.

# 21. Independent vs Dependent Events

  

This is central to the slides.

  

## Independent

  

Knowing A happened gives no new information about B.

  

Mathematically:

  

$$

\boxed{P(B\mid A)=P(B)}

$$

  

and equivalently:

  

$$

\boxed{P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)}

$$

  

Example:

  

- coin result

- die result.

  

Knowing the coin was heads doesn't change the die.

  

---

  

# 22. Dependent

  

If A happening changes B's probability:

  

$$

P(B\mid A)\neq P(B)

$$

  

Then:

  

$$

\boxed{ P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B\mid A) }

$$

  

Your lecture states both forms:

  

$$

P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B\mid A)

$$

  

or:

  

$$

P(A\cap B)=P(B)P(A\mid B)

$$

  
  
  

---

  

# 23. Conditional Probability

  

This is used throughout the file even before being separately emphasized.

  

$$

\boxed{P(B\mid A)}

$$

  

reads:

  

> Probability of B **given that A has happened**.

  

The general formula is:

  

$$

\boxed{P(B\mid A)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(A)}}

$$

  

provided $P(A)>0$.

  

Rearrange:

  

$$

P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B\mid A)

$$

  

which is exactly the multiplication rule for dependent events.

# 24. Intuition Behind "Given"

  

This is one of the most important probability concepts.

  

Suppose:

  

- 100 students total

- 40 use the bus

- 10 both use the bus and are late.

  

Then:

  

$$

P(\text{late}\mid\text{bus})

$$

  

does **not** use all 100 students as denominator anymore.

  

Once you are told:

  

> Given that the student uses the bus,

  

your new universe contains only the 40 bus users.

  

Among those 40, 10 are late.

  

Therefore:

  

$$

P(L\mid B)=\frac{10}{40}=\frac14

$$

  

This leads to a fundamental rule:

  

> **The event after the vertical bar tells you the new denominator/universe.**

  

$$

P(A\mid B)

$$

  

means:

  

> restrict attention to B first.

# 30. An Extremely Important Distinction: Independence vs Mutually Exclusive

  

This is not emphasized enough in many slides, but you should know it.

  

## Mutually exclusive

  

A and B **cannot happen together**.

  

Therefore:

  

$$

\boxed{P(A\cap B)=0}

$$

  

Example with one die roll:

  

$$

A=\{\text{roll 2}\}

$$

  

$$

B=\{\text{roll 5}\}

$$

  

They cannot both occur.

  

---

  

## Independent

  

A happening does not affect B.

  

$$

P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)

$$

  

Example:

  

- coin = H

- die = 5.

  

They absolutely **can happen together**.

  

---

  

## Very important consequence

  

If A and B are mutually exclusive and both have positive probability, they are **not independent**.

  

Why?

  

If A occurs, B becomes impossible:

  

$$

P(B\mid A)=0

$$

  

while normally:

  

$$

P(B)>0

$$

  

So knowing A happened changes B's probability dramatically.

It means the probability that both events happen together.

  

"Joint" simply means:

  

> involving more than one variable/event simultaneously.

A useful related concept:

  

### With replacement

  

Draw something, put it back.

  

Composition is unchanged.

  

Typically independent draws.

  

### Without replacement

  

Do not put it back.

  

Composition changes.

  

Typically dependent draws.

Without replacement:

  

$$

$$

$$

P(R_2\mid R_1)=\frac24

$$

  

So the probability changed.

# 42. Lecture 4 — Joint Probability Tables

  

Now the lecture shifts from formulas into tables.

  

A $2\times2$ joint probability table has this structure:

  

|  | $Y$ | $\bar Y$ | Total |

|---|---:|---:|---:|

| $X$ | $P(X\cap Y)$ | $P(X\cap\bar Y)$ | $P(X)$ |

| $\bar X$ | $P(\bar X\cap Y)$ | $P(\bar X\cap\bar Y)$ | $P(\bar X)$ |

| Total | $P(Y)$ | $P(\bar Y)$ | $1$ |

  

This is exactly the conceptual table shown on page 8 of the slides: each inner cell is the joint probability of the corresponding row and column event.

  

---

  

# 43. Why the Table Is So Useful

  

The table physically shows the four regions we discussed earlier:

  

$$

X\cap Y

$$

  

$$

X\cap\bar Y

$$

  

$$

\bar X\cap Y

$$

  

$$

\bar X\cap\bar Y

$$

  

Row totals give probabilities of the row variables.

  

Column totals give probabilities of the column variables.

  

All four internal cells add to:

  

$$

1

$$

  

---

  

# 44. How to Fill a (2\times2) Table

  

Suppose:

  

$$

P(X)=0.7

$$

  

$$

P(Y)=0.6

$$

  

$$

P(X\cap Y)=0.4

$$

  

Start with intersection:

  

|  | $Y$ | $\bar Y$ | Total |

|---|---:|---:|---:|

| $X$ | 0.4 | ? | 0.7 |

| $\bar X$ | ? | ? | 0.3 |

| Total | 0.6 | 0.4 | 1 |

  

Now use subtraction.

  

Top-right:

  

$$

0.7-0.4=0.3

$$

  

Bottom-left:

  

$$

0.6-0.4=0.2

$$

  

Bottom-right:

  

$$

1-(0.4+0.3+0.2)=0.1

$$

  

So:

  

|  | $Y$ | $\bar Y$ | Total |

|---|---:|---:|---:|

| $X$ | 0.4 | 0.3 | 0.7 |

| $\bar X$ | 0.2 | 0.1 | 0.3 |

| Total | 0.6 | 0.4 | 1 |

  

This systematic method is much safer than jumping between formulas.

$$

  

$$

$$

  

$$

$$

\text{neither}=n(S)-n(A\cup B)

$$

  

That fills the entire (2\times2) table.

# 51. Machine A/B Problem — Very Important

# 52. Turn Conditional Probability Into Joint Probability

  

The statement:

  

> 5% of items **from Machine A** are defective

  

means:

  

$$

P(D\mid A)=0.05

$$

  

It does **not** mean:

  

$$

P(A\cap D)=0.05

$$

  

This distinction is essential.

  

To get the joint probability:

  

$$

P(A\cap D)=P(A)P(D\mid A)

$$

  

$$

0.4(0.05)=\boxed{0.02}

$$

  

Similarly:

  

$$

P(B\cap D)=0.6(0.10)=\boxed{0.06}

$$

# 53. Non-Defective Probabilities

  

If:

  

$$

P(D\mid A)=0.05

$$

  

then:

  

$$

P(\bar D\mid A)=0.95

$$

  

Therefore:

  

$$

P(A\cap\bar D)=0.4(0.95)=0.38

$$

  

For B:

  

$$

P(\bar D\mid B)=0.90

$$

  

so:

  

$$

P(B\cap\bar D)=0.6(0.90)=0.54

$$

  

The resulting table is exactly what the slide gives.

|---|---:|---:|---:|

| A | 0.02 | 0.38 | 0.40 |

# 54. Probability an Item Is Defective

  

There are two ways an item can be defective:

  

$$

A\cap D

$$

  

or:

  

$$

B\cap D

$$

  

These cases cannot occur simultaneously because an item came from one machine.

  

So:

  

$$

P(D)=P(A\cap D)+P(B\cap D)

$$

  

$$

0.02+0.06

$$

  

$$

\boxed{0.08}

$$

# 55. This Is the Law of Total Probability

  

Your slide performs it but does not really emphasize the name.

  

Because every item comes from either A or B:

  

$$

\boxed{P(D)=P(D\mid A)P(A)+P(D\mid B)P(B)}

$$

  

Here:

  

$$

0.05(0.4)+0.10(0.6)

$$

  

$$

0.02+0.06=\boxed{0.08}

$$

  

This is called the **Law of Total Probability**.

  

You should definitely know it because it is the natural next concept from these slides.

# 56. Machine A Given That Item Is Defective

  

Now the question reverses the conditioning.

  

Originally we know:

  

$$

P(D\mid A)

$$

  

But now we want:

  

$$

P(A\mid D)

$$

  

These are not the same.

  

Use conditional probability:

  

$$

P(A\mid D)=\frac{P(A\cap D)}{P(D)}

$$

  

$$

\frac{0.02}{0.08}

$$

  

$$

\boxed{0.25}

$$

  

The slide obtains the same result.

  

---

  

# 57. This Is Bayes' Theorem

  

The previous expression can be written:

  

$$

P(A\mid D)=\frac{P(D\mid A)P(A)}{P(D)}

$$

  

and because:

  

$$

P(D)=P(D\mid A)P(A)+P(D\mid B)P(B)

$$

  

we get:

  

$$

\boxed{P(A\mid D)=\frac{P(D\mid A)P(A)}{P(D\mid A)P(A)+P(D\mid B)P(B)}}

$$

  

That is **Bayes' theorem**.

  

This is the single most relevant extra concept to learn after these slides.

  

---

  

# 58. Why Bayes Feels Like "Reversing" a Condition

  

You know:

  

$$

P(D\mid A)

$$

  

> Among Machine A items, how many are defective?

  

But Bayes asks:

  

$$

P(A\mid D)

$$

  

> Among defective items, how many came from A?

  

The denominator population changed.

  

This is why:

  

$$

P(A\mid D)\neq P(D\mid A)

$$

  

in general.

# 62. Joint Probability Function (f(x,y))

  

The joint PMF tells us:

  

$$

\boxed{ f(x,y)=P(X=x,Y=y) }

$$

  

For example:

  

$$

f(1,0)=P(\text{exactly 1 blue, 0 red})

$$

  

If 2 pens are selected and there is exactly 1 blue and 0 red, the other pen must be green.

  

So:

  

$$

P(X=1,Y=0) \frac{ \binom31\binom20\binom31 }{ \binom82 }

$$

  

$$

\frac{9}{28}

$$

# 63. General Formula for This Problem

  

There are:

  

- 3 blue

- 2 red

- 3 green.

  

If:

  

$$

X=x,\qquad Y=y

$$

  

then the number of green pens selected must be:

  

$$

2-x-y

$$

  

Therefore:

  

$$

\boxed{f(x,y)=\frac{\binom3x\binom2y\binom3{2-x-y}}{\binom82}}

$$

  

provided:

  

$$

x\ge0,\quad y\ge0,\quad x+y\le2

$$

  

and impossible combinations have probability 0.

  

This formula is a very useful way to understand the entire table rather than calculating cells randomly.

# 68. Marginal Probability Distribution

  

This is a major concept in the last slide.

  

The **joint distribution** knows about X and Y together:

  

$$

\boxed{ P(X=x)=\sum_y f(x,y) }

This is called the **marginal distribution of X**.

  

Similarly:

  

$$

\boxed{ P(Y=y)=\sum_x f(x,y) }

$$

# 70. Joint vs Marginal vs Conditional — Do Not Mix These

  

This distinction is extremely important.

  

## Joint

  

$$

P(X=x,Y=y)

$$

  

Means:

  

> X has value x **and** Y has value y.

  

Example:

  

$$

P(X=1,Y=0)=\frac9{28}

$$

  

---

  

## Marginal

  

$$

P(X=x)

$$

  

Means:

  

> only care about X; Y can be anything.

  

So:

  

$$

P(X=1)=\sum_y P(X=1,Y=y)

$$

  

---

  

## Conditional

  

$$

P(X=x\mid Y=y)

$$

  

Means:

  

> restrict the universe to cases where $Y=y$, then examine X.

  

Formula:

  

$$

\boxed{P(X=x\mid Y=y)=\frac{P(X=x,Y=y)}{P(Y=y)}}

$$

  

These three quantities are related but not interchangeable.

# 72. Testing Independence From a Joint Table

  

This is a closely related skill worth learning.

  

X and Y are independent if:

  

$$

\boxed{P(X=x,Y=y)=P(X=x)P(Y=y)}

$$

  

for every possible $(x,y)$.

  

For example, test:

  

$$

X=0,Y=0

$$

  

Joint:

  

$$

P(X=0,Y=0)=\frac3{28}

$$

  

Marginals:

  

$$

P(X=0)=\frac{10}{28}

$$

  

$$

P(Y=0)=\frac{15}{28}

$$

  

If independent, we would require:

  

$$

\frac3{28}=\frac{10}{28}\frac{15}{28}

$$

  

But these are not equal.

  

So:

  

$$

\boxed{X\text{ and }Y\text{ are dependent}}

$$

  

That makes intuitive sense: if you draw more blue pens, fewer slots remain available for red pens.

# 73. Your Master Methodology for These Lectures

  

When you face a problem, don't immediately hunt for a formula. Translate the wording first.

  

| Wording | Think |

|---|---|

| both | intersection $A\cap B$ |

| A and B | intersection |

| A or B | union |

| at least one | union or $1-P(\text{neither})$ |

| neither | $\bar A\cap\bar B$ |

| exactly one | X-only + Y-only |

| A but not B | $A\cap\bar B$ |

| given A | conditional probability; A becomes denominator universe |

| independent | multiply ordinary probabilities |

| dependent | multiply by conditional probability |

| odds in favor | success : failure |

| odds against | failure : success |

| joint | row AND column simultaneously |

| marginal | add across other variable |

| probability of a region | add all joint-table cells in that region |

# 74. A Decision Tree to Use in Exams

  

Suppose the question asks about two events A and B.

  

First translate the sentence.

  

If it says **both**:

  

$$

P(A\cap B)

$$

  

Then ask: independent?

  

If yes:

  

$$

P(A)P(B)

$$

  

If no:

  

$$

P(A)P(B\mid A)

$$

  

---

  

If it says **at least one / A or B**:

  

$$

P(A\cup B)

$$

  

Use:

  

$$

P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B)

$$

  

or:

  

$$

1-P(\bar A\cap\bar B)

$$

  

whichever is easier.

  

---

  

If it says **neither**:

  

$$

1-P(A\cup B)

$$

  

---

  

If it says **exactly one**:

  

$$

P(A\cup B)-P(A\cap B)

$$

  

or:

  

$$

P(A\cap\bar B)+P(\bar A\cap B)

$$

  

---

  

If it says **A given B**:

  

$$

\boxed{P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}}

$$

  

and remember:

  

> B is now your universe.

# 75. A Formula Map Worth Memorizing

  

These formulas are heavily interconnected:

  

$$

\boxed{P(\bar A)=1-P(A)}

$$

  

$$

\boxed{ P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B) }

$$

  

$$

\boxed{ P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)} }

$$

  

$$

\boxed{ P(A\cap B)=P(B)P(A\mid B) }

$$

  

If independent:

  

$$

\boxed{ P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B) }

$$

  

Bayes:

  

$$

\boxed{P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(B\mid A)P(A)}{P(B)}}

$$

  

Total probability, when (A\_1,\ldots,A\_n) partition the sample space:

  

$$

\boxed{P(B)=\sum_i P(B\mid A_i)P(A_i)}

$$

  

Joint-to-marginal:

  

$$

\boxed{ P(X=x)=\sum_yP(X=x,Y=y) }

$$

  

These are really one connected system rather than eight unrelated formulas.

$$

  

|  | Facebook | Not Facebook | Total |

|---|---:|---:|---:|

| Instagram | 60 | 30 | 90 |

| Not Instagram | 60 | 50 | 110 |

| Total | 120 | 80 | 200 |

  

Then:

  

$$

P(F\mid I)=\frac{60}{90}=\boxed{\frac23}

$$

  

Notice the denominator is 90, because we are **given Instagram**.

# 82. Practice — Bayes

  

A disease occurs in:

  

$$

1%

$$

  

of people.

  

A test is positive for:

  

$$

90%

$$

  

of diseased people.

  

It also gives a false positive to:

  

$$

5%

$$

  

of healthy people.

  

Find:

  

$$

P(D\mid +)

$$

  

Let:

  

$$

P(D)=0.01

$$

  

$$

P(\bar D)=0.99

$$

  

$$

P(+\mid D)=0.90

$$

  

$$

P(+\mid\bar D)=0.05

$$

  

First find total positive probability:

  

$$

P(+)=0.90(0.01)+0.05(0.99)

$$

  

$$

0.009+0.0495=0.0585

$$

  

Bayes:

  

$$

P(D\mid+)=\frac{0.90(0.01)}{0.0585}

$$

  

$$

\frac{0.009}{0.0585} \approx \boxed{0.1538}

$$

  

Only about:

  

$$

\boxed{15.4%}

$$

  

Despite the test being "90% positive for diseased people".

  

This is a classic demonstration that:

  

$$

P(+\mid D)

$$

  

and:

  

$$

P(D\mid+)

$$

  

are completely different questions.

## The single mental picture I would keep

  

For two binary events, imagine this complete table:

  

|  | $B$ | $\bar B$ | Total |

|---|---|---|---:|

| $A$ | **both** | **A only** | $P(A)$ |

| $\bar A$ | **B only** | **neither** | $P(\bar A)$ |

| Total | $P(B)$ | $P(\bar B)$ | 1 |

  

Then almost every phrase in these lectures becomes visible:

  

- **both** → top-left

- **A but not B** → top-right

- **B but not A** → bottom-left

- **neither** → bottom-right

- **exactly one** → top-right + bottom-left

- **at least one** → everything except bottom-right

- **A given B** → among the B column, what fraction is in A

- **B given A** → among the A row, what fraction is in B

- **joint** → one internal cell

- **marginal** → a row/column total

  

If that table becomes intuitive, most of Lecture 3–4 stops being a formula-memorization exercise.

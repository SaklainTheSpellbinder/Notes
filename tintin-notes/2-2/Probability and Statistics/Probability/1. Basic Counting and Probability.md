
> **Do not start by asking “Which formula should I use?”**  
> First ask: **“What does one valid outcome look like, and how can I construct it?”**

Once that becomes habitual, permutation/combination problems become much less arbitrary.

# Part 1 — Random Experiments

Your slide defines a **random experiment** as something where:

- all possible outcomes are known,
    
- you cannot know beforehand exactly which one will happen,
    
- and the experiment can be repeated under identical conditions.
    

For example, toss a coin:

$$  
S=\{H,T\}  
$$

You know those are the only possibilities. You simply do not know which one will occur before the toss.

Similarly:

$$  
\text{Die: }S=\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}  
$$

A very important term here is **sample space**.

$$  
\boxed{S=\text{set of all possible outcomes}}  
$$

And an **event** is some subset of that sample space.

Example:

$$  
S=\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}  
$$

Let

$$  
A=\{\text{even number}\}=\{2,4,6\}  
$$

Then $A$ is an event.

---

# Part 2 — Equally Likely Outcomes

Two outcomes/events are equally likely when they have the same chance of occurring. The slides give the examples of head vs tail for a fair coin and getting 1 vs 2 on a fair die.

For a fair die:

$$  
P(1)=P(2)=\cdots=P(6)=\frac16  $$

This matters because the classical probability formula

$$  
P(A)=\frac{\text{favorable outcomes}}{\text{total outcomes}}  
$$

only works directly when the elementary outcomes being counted are equally likely.

# Part 3 — Sets and De Morgan's Laws

Your slides give:

$$  
(A\cup B)^c=A^c\cap B^c  
$$

and

$$  
(A\cap B)^c=A^c\cup B^c  $$

These may look like abstract set formulas, but they become extremely useful in counting.

## Understand the words first

$$  
A\cup B  
$$

means:

> A **or** B or both.

$$  
A\cap B  
$$

means:

> A **and** B.

$$  
A^c  
$$

means:

> not A.

So:

$$  
(A\cup B)^c  
$$

means:

> neither A nor B.

which is the same as:

> not A **and** not B.

Therefore:

$$
(A\cup B)^c=A^c\cap B^c  
$$

---

# Why De Morgan matters enormously for "at least one"

Suppose you toss 10 coins and want:

> At least one head.

Direct counting means:

- exactly 1 head,
    
- exactly 2,
    
- exactly 3,
    
- ...
    
- exactly 10.
    

That's annoying.

Instead:

$$  
P(\text{at least one H})
=
1-P(\text{no H})  
$$

"No head" means every toss is T.

So:

$$
P(\ge1H)=1-\left(\frac12\right)^{10}  
$$

This is basically De Morgan's law being used as a counting strategy.

Whenever you see:

> **at least one**

your brain should immediately consider:

$$
\boxed{\text{Total}-\text{None}}  
$$

Before permutation and combination, these are actually the most fundamental tools.

## 4.1 Multiplication principle — "AND / then"

Suppose you have:

- 4 shirts
    
- 3 trousers
    

For every shirt, there are 3 trouser choices.

Therefore:

$$
4\times3=12  
$$

outfits.

Think:

> choose shirt **AND THEN** choose trousers.

Multiply.

## 4.2 Addition principle — "OR"

Suppose a restaurant offers:

- 5 burgers
    
- 4 pizzas
    

and you will order **one burger OR one pizza**.

Then:

$$
5+4=9  
$$

options.

But this simple addition works when the cases don't overlap.

If overlap exists:

$$
\boxed{n(A\cup B)=n(A)+n(B)-n(A\cap B)}  
$$

We'll use this later.

n!=n(n-1)(n-2)\cdots2\cdot1  
$$

For example:

$$
5!=5\times4\times3\times2\times1=120

0!=1

# Part 6 — Permutation

Your slide defines permutation as an arrangement where **order matters** and gives

$$
n_{P_r}=\frac{n!}{(n-r)!}  
$$

The intuitive reason is more useful than memorizing the formula.

Suppose 5 students compete for:

- president
    
- vice-president
    
- secretary.
    

For president:

$$
5  
$$

choices.

Then VP:

$$
4  
$$

Then secretary:

$$
3  
$$

Therefore:

$$
5\times4\times3=60  
$$

which is:

$$
{}_5P_3=60  
$$

---

# The easiest permutation test

Ask:

> If I swap two chosen objects, have I created a different answer?

President = Alice, VP = Bob

is different from

President = Bob, VP = Alice.

So:
$$  
\boxed{\text{order matters}}  
$$

Therefore permutation.

# Part 7 — Combination

Your slide defines a combination as a selection where **order does not matter**:

$$  
{}_nC_r=\frac{n!}{r!(n-r)!}  
$$

Suppose we select 3 students from 10 for a committee.

Committee:

$$
\{A,B,C\}  
$$

is exactly the same committee as

$$
\{C,A,B\}  
$$

So order does not matter.

Therefore:

$$
\binom{10}{3}  
$$

---

# Why combination divides by (r!)

If you use permutation:

$$  
{}_nP_r  
$$

you count every selected group many times.

For three students (A,B,C):

$$  
ABC,ACB,BAC,BCA,CAB,CBA  
$$

are six arrangements.

But as a committee, all six are the same selection.

There are:

$$
3!=6  
$$

orders.

Therefore:

$$  
\binom nr=\frac{{}_nP_r}{r!}  
$$

are men.

# Part 10 — "At Least"

This causes much more confusion.

"At least 2" means:

$$  
2,3,4,5,\ldots  
$$

not exactly 2.

For a committee of 5 containing at least 2 women:

possible women counts:

$$  
2,3,4,5  
$$

# Method A — break into cases

Suppose:

- 6 men
    
- 4 women
    
- committee of 5
    
- at least 2 women.
    

Cases:

### 2 women, 3 men

$$  
\binom42\binom63  
$$

### 3 women, 2 men

$$
\binom43\binom62  
$$

### 4 women, 1 man

$$  
\binom44\binom61  
$$

Then ADD:

$$
\boxed{  
\binom42\binom63+  
\binom43\binom62+  
\binom44\binom61  
}  
$$

Why add?

Because a committee cannot simultaneously contain exactly 2 women and exactly 3 women.

These cases are mutually exclusive.

---

# Method B — complement

If the restriction says:

> at least one woman

the complement is beautifully simple:

> zero women.

Your slide uses exactly this method for 6 men, 4 women, committee of 5.

Total:

$$  
\binom{10}{5}  
$$

No woman:

$$  
\binom65  
$$

Therefore:

$$
\binom{10}{5}-\binom65  
$$

$$
252-6  
$$

$$  
\boxed{246}  
$$

---

# When should you use complement?

Whenever the unwanted situation is much easier than the wanted situation.

Especially:

- at least one
    
- not all
    
- not together
    
- at least one repeated
    
- at least one from category X
    
- no restriction except one forbidden situation
    

Think:

$$  
\boxed{\text{wanted}=\text{total}-\text{unwanted}}  
$$

# Part 11 — "At Most"

"At most 2" means:

$$  
0,1,2  
$$

"At most 1" means:

$$
0,1  
$$

"At most 5" means:

$$
0,1,2,3,4,5  
$$

This wording must become automatic.

For example:

8 people consisting of 5 men and 3 women. Select 4 people with at most 1 woman.

Cases:

### 0 women

$$
\binom50\binom54=\binom54  
$$

More naturally:

$$
\binom54  
$$

### 1 woman

$$
\binom31\binom53  
$$

Therefore:

$$  
\boxed{\binom54+\binom31\binom53}  
$$

# Part 14 — Classical Probability

The slide gives:

$$  
\boxed{P(A)=\frac{n(A)}{n(S)}}  
$$

where (n(A)) is favorable outcomes and (n(S)) is total possible outcomes.

The important hidden issue is:

> Your numerator and denominator must count outcomes in the **same way**.

This mistake appears constantly in card problems.

For a five-card hand:

denominator:

$$
\binom{52}{5}  
$$

So the numerator must also count **unordered five-card hands**.

Don't suddenly use permutations upstairs.

---

# Example

Probability that a 5-card hand contains exactly 1 ace:

$$  
P=  
\frac{\binom41\binom{48}{4}}  
{\binom{52}{5}}  
$$

That is the cleanest solution.

# Part 15 — Relative Frequency and Subjective Probability

The slide lists three approaches:

1. classical
    
2. relative frequency
    
3. subjective
    

but only develops the classical approach in this lecture.

For completeness, standard probability terminology is:

### Relative frequency

Estimate probability from repeated observations:

$$  
P(A)\approx  
\frac{\text{number of times A occurred}}  
{\text{number of trials}}  
$$

Example: a machine failed 18 times in 1000 runs:

$$
P(\text{failure})\approx0.018  
$$

### Subjective probability

Probability based on informed judgment rather than equally likely counting or repeated experimental data.

For example, an analyst may estimate a 30% probability that a particular project misses its deadline.

These definitions are extra background; they are not elaborated in your uploaded lecture.

# Part 17 — Independent Multiplication Rule

The lecture states:

$$
\boxed{P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)}  
$$

when (A) and (B) are independent.

Independent means:

> knowing that A happened does not change the probability of B.

Example:

- toss coin
    
- roll die.
    

Getting H does not alter the die.

So:

$$ 
P(H\text{ and }4)

= \frac12\times\frac16

=\boxed{\frac1{12}}  
$$

---

# Part 18 — Dependent Events

For dependent events:

$$
\boxed{  
P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B\mid A)  
}  
$$

Example from your slide:

Bag:

- 3 red
    
- 2 blue.
    

Draw two **without replacement**.

First red:

$$
P(R_1)=\frac35  
$$

After drawing a red:

- 2 red
    
- 2 blue remain.
    

Therefore:

$$  
P(R_2\mid R_1)=\frac24  
$$

Thus:
$$ 
P(R_1\cap R_2)

= \frac35\times\frac24

=\boxed{\frac3{10}}  
$$

The slide also shows the equivalent counting approach:

$$  
\frac{\binom32}{\binom52}  
=\frac3{10}  
$$

Ask:

> Is there an order/position/job/ranking?

$$
\boxed{\_\quad \_\quad \_\quad \_}

This is often much easier than trying to remember a formula.

# Part 19 — Fixed Object at a Fixed Position

Suppose 8 distinct books are arranged on a shelf and book A must be first.

Normally:

$$ 
8!  
$$

But A is fixed:

$$
\boxed{A} \  \_ \ \_ \ \_ \ \_ \ \_ \ \_ \ \_  
$$

Only 7 objects remain.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{7!}  
$$

---

# Two objects fixed

A must be first and B must be last:

$$  
A\;\_\;\_\;\_\;\_\;\_\;\_\;B  
$$

Remaining 6:

$$
\boxed{6!}  
$$

General rule:

If $k$ specified distinct objects are locked into $k$ specified positions among $n$ objects:

$$
\boxed{(n-k)!}  
$$

> vowels in odd positions.

- 3 distinct consonants
    

and all 7 are arranged so that vowels must occupy odd positions.

Positions:

$$

Odd positions:

$$
1,3,5,7

Exactly four positions.

$$
4!

\boxed{4!3!}

# More general version

Suppose:

- 5 vowels
    
- 6 consonants
    
- arrange 8 letters
    
- exactly 3 vowels must occupy odd positions.
    

Now you need to think more carefully.

If the objects are distinct:

1. choose which 3 vowels:
    

$$
\binom53  
$$

2. choose which 3 allowed odd positions they occupy, depending on how many odd positions are available.
    
3. arrange them:
    

$$
3!  
$$

4. fill remaining positions from permissible objects.
    

The main idea:

> When a **group of objects** can occupy a **group of positions**, you often choose the objects, choose the positions, then arrange.

That pattern appears constantly.

# Part 22 — "A and B Must Be Together" — Block Method

Suppose 6 people:

$$
A,B,C,D,E,F  
$$

are arranged in a row, with A and B together.

Treat:

$$
[AB]  
$$

as one object.

Now you have:

$$
[AB],C,D,E,F  
$$

= 5 objects.

Arrange:

$$
5!  
$$

But inside the block:

$$
AB,\ BA  
$$

= (2!) arrangements.

Thus:

$$  
\boxed{5!\times2!}  
$$

---

# Three specified objects together

Suppose A, B, C must stay together among 8 people.

$$ 
[ABC]  
$$

Together with the other five people gives:

$$  
6  
$$

units.

Arrange units:

$$  
6!  
$$

Arrange ABC internally:

$$

$$

$$  
\boxed{6!\times3!}

$$
\boxed{(n-r+1)!r!}  
$$

# Part 23 — "A and B Must NOT Be Together"

This is where complement is easier.

For 6 people:

Total:

$$
6!  
$$

A and B together:

$$  
5!2!  
$$

Therefore not together:

$$  
\boxed{6!-5!2!}  
$$

# Part 24 — No Two Members of a Group Adjacent

This is the **gap method**.

Suppose:

- 5 boys
    
- 3 girls
    

must stand in a row so that no two girls are adjacent.

First arrange boys:

$$  
B\quad B\quad B\quad B\quad B  
$$

They create gaps:

$$ 
\_B\_B\_B\_B\_B\_  
$$

There are:

$$
6  
$$

gaps.

To stop girls being adjacent, use at most one girl per gap.

Choose 3 gaps:

$$  
\binom63  
$$

Arrange girls:

$$
3!  
$$

Arrange boys:

$$
5!  
$$

Therefore:

$$  
\boxed{5!\binom63 3!}  
$$

This is one of the most useful patterns in all elementary counting.

# Part 28 — Repeated Objects

Suppose letters of:

$$
\text{BANANA}  
$$

There are 6 letters:

- A repeated 3 times
    
- N repeated 2 times
    
- B once.
    

If all were distinct:

$$  
6!  
$$

But swapping identical A's does not create a new arrangement.

Divide by:

$$
3!  
$$

Likewise N:

$$  
2!  
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{\frac{6!}{3!2!}}  
$$

$$  
\boxed{  
\frac{n!}{n_{1}! n_{2}! \cdots n_{k}!}
}
$$

where:

$$ 
n_1+n_2+\cdots+n_k=n  
$$

# Part 30 — Circular Permutation

For $n$ distinct people around a round table:

$$  
\boxed{(n-1)!}  
$$

Why not $n!$?

Because rotating everybody together doesn't create a genuinely different seating.

Fix one person as an anchor.

Then arrange the remaining (n-1):

$$
(n-1)!  
$$

Example: 6 people around a table:

$$
\boxed{5!}  
$$

---

# Two people together around a circle

6 people, A and B together.

Treat AB as a block.

Now there are 5 circular units:

$$  
(5-1)!=4!  
$$

Internal order:

$$
2!  
$$

Answer:

$$
\boxed{4!2!}  
$$

# Part 31 — Inclusion-Exclusion

You already know the two-set formula:

$$  
|A\cup B|

=|A|+|B|-|A\cap B|  
$$

Why subtract the intersection?

Because elements in both A and B got counted twice.

Example:

Among 100 students:

- 60 study Java
    
- 45 study Python
    
- 20 study both.
    

Number studying at least one:

# Part 35 — Consecutive Objects: Specified Objects

# But there's a completely different problem:

> At least 3 men must be consecutive.

That does **not** mean some specified three men form one block.

Any three or more men may create a run.

These problems require more care.

This is where the **gap/run method** becomes useful.

# Part 36 — At Most $r$ Consecutive Objects of One Type

Suppose you have:

- 5 objects of type A
    
- 3 objects of type B
    

and want arrangements containing **at most 2 consecutive A's**.

First arrange the B-type positions conceptually:

$$
\_B\_B\_B\_  
$$

There are 4 gaps.

Put A's into those gaps.

Let the numbers of A's be:

$$
x_1,x_2,x_3,x_4  
$$

Then:

$$
x_1+x_2+x_3+x_4=5  
$$

Because at most 2 A's can occur consecutively:

$$
0\le x_i\le2  
$$

So the whole problem becomes:

> How many solutions does

$$
x_1+x_2+x_3+x_4=5  
$$

have where every $x_i\le2$?

This is the deeper version of the gap method.

---

# Count it

Ignoring the upper restriction temporarily:

$$
x_1+x_2+x_3+x_4=5  
$$
This is solved using a classic combinatorics theorem called **Stars and Bars**. The formula to distribute $n$ identical items into $k$ distinct bins is:

$$\binom{n+k-1}{k-1}$$
Plugging in our numbers (5 items, 4 bins):

$$ 
\binom{5+4-1}{4-1}

= \binom83

=56  
$$

solutions.

Now subtract solutions where some $x_i\ge3$.

An arrangement breaks the rule if **any gap gets 3 or more A's** (meaning $x_i \ge 3$).

To count how many times this happens, we force a gap to break the rule:

1. **Choose the offending gap:** There are 4 gaps to choose from. Let's pick one to be our "rule breaker".
    
2. **Pre-fill the gap:** We immediately drop 3 A's into that chosen gap to guarantee it breaks the rule. (This is what the text means by $y_i = x_i - 3$).
    
3. **Distribute the rest:** We started with 5 A's, and we just spent 3 of them to break the rule. We only have 2 A's left to distribute among the 4 gaps.

Choose offending gap:

$$
4  
$$

Let:

$$
y_i=x_i-3  
$$

Remaining sum:

$$
2  
$$

Number of solutions: with Stars and Bars for the remaining 2 items in 4 bins:

$$ 
\binom{2+4-1}{3}

= \binom53

=10  
$$

For _any_ gap we pick to be the rule breaker, there are 10 ways to distribute the remaining A's. Since there are 4 gaps that could potentially be the rule breaker, we multiply:

4 gaps $\times$ 10 ways = **40 bad scenarios**.

(Note: Sometimes we have to worry about counting double-rule-breakers twice. But because it takes 3 A's to break a rule, and we only have 5 A's total, it is impossible for two gaps to break the rule at the same time. 3 + 3 = 6, which is more A's than we have!)

So subtract:

$$ 
4(10)=40  
$$

Two gaps cannot both contain at least 3 because that would require at least 6 A's.

Therefore:

$$
56-40=16  
$$

So there are:

$$
\boxed{16}  
$$

type-patterns.

Up until this point, we treated all A's as identical clones of each other, and all B's as identical clones.

If the problem states that the 5 A's are actually distinct individuals (like 5 different men) and the 3 B's are distinct (like 3 different women), we have to account for how they can be swapped around within their assigned slots.

- The 5 distinct A's can be shuffled in $5!$ ways.
    
- The 3 distinct B's can be shuffled in $3!$ ways.

If all 5 A's are distinct and all 3 B's distinct, multiply:

$$
\boxed{16(5!)(3!)}  
$$

This is an advanced technique, but it gives you a systematic way to handle "at most (r) consecutive".

---

# Part 37 — At Least $r$ Consecutive

Using the same example:

5 A's + 3 B's.

Want **at least 3 consecutive A's**.

Rather than directly count all possible runs:

$$ 
AAA,\ AAAA,\ AAAAA  
$$

use complement.

Total A/B patterns:

$$
\binom85=56  
$$

Patterns having **no 3 consecutive A's** are exactly patterns with at most 2 consecutive A's.

We just found:

$$
16  
$$

Therefore:

$$ 
56-16

=\boxed{40}  
$$

So:

$$ 
\boxed{\text{at least 3 consecutive}=  
\text{total}-\text{at most 2 consecutive}}  
$$

This is a very important relationship.

---

# Part 38 — Exactly a Maximum Run of $r$

Suppose the question means:

> the longest consecutive run of A's is exactly 2.

Then:

$$
\boxed{  
N(\text{max run}\le2)
-
N(\text{max run}\le1)  
}  
$$

Generally:
$$  
\boxed{  
N(\text{maximum run exactly }r)
=
 N(\text{maximum run}\le r)
-
N(\text{maximum run}\le r-1)  
}  
$$

This is much safer than trying to draw cases manually.

---

# Part 39 — Stars and Bars

The previous problem introduced another common counting method.

Suppose 7 identical candies are distributed among 3 students with no restriction.

Let:

$$  
x_1+x_2+x_3=7  
$$

where $x_i\ge0$.

Number of solutions:

$$
\boxed{  
\binom{7+3-1}{3-1}
=
\binom92  
}  
$$

General rule:

$$ 
x_1+x_2+\cdots+x_k=n,\qquad x_i\ge0  
$$

has:

$$ 
\boxed{\binom{n+k-1}{k-1}}  
$$

solutions.

---

# If everybody must receive at least one

$$
x_i\ge1  
$$

Give one to everyone first.

Suppose:

$$
x_1+x_2+x_3=7  
$$

with each $\ge1$.

Remove 1 each:

$$ 
y_1+y_2+y_3=4  
$$

with $y_i\ge0$.

So:

$$
\boxed{\binom62}  
$$

General:

$$  
x_1+\cdots+x_k=n,\quad x_i\ge1  
$$

gives:

$$  
\boxed{\binom{n-1}{k-1}}  
$$

---

# Part 40 — Fixed Lower Bounds

Suppose:

$$  
x_1+x_2+x_3=15  
$$

and:

$$
x_1\ge2,\quad x_2\ge3,\quad x_3\ge1  
$$

Give away the mandatory amounts:

$$  
2+3+1=6  
$$

Remaining:

$$  
15-6=9  
$$

Let:

$$  
y_1+y_2+y_3=9  
$$

Therefore:

$$  
\boxed{\binom{11}{2}}  
$$

This technique is often hidden inside words like:

> at least 2 items for A, at least 3 for B...

# Part 41 — Derangements: Nobody in Their Own Position

This is another "fixed position" family worth knowing.

Suppose 4 students submit four assignments. The assignments are redistributed so nobody receives their own.

This is called a **derangement**.

For (n) objects:

$$ 
!n
=
n!
\left(  
1-\frac1{1!}+\frac1{2!}-\frac1{3!}+\cdots+  
\frac{(-1)^n}{n!}  
\right)  
$$

For 4:

$$  
!4=9  
$$

You may not need the formula in this particular lecture, but recognize the phrase:

> none of the objects may occupy its original position.

That is a derangement/inclusion-exclusion problem.

# The Master Counting Methodology

For an exam question, literally work through this sequence.

> One valid outcome is ______.

Example:

- 1 woman.

N_0+N_1  
$$

Then:

$$

## Question 6 — Is there a position restriction?

Draw actual slots:

Lock restricted positions first.

## Question 7 — Do some objects have to stay together?

Make a block.

## Question 8 — Must objects be separated?

Use gaps.

## OR → addition

exactly 2 women **or** exactly 3 women:

# A Very Common Mistake With "OR"

Suppose a die is rolled.

A = even:

$$  
{2,4,6}  
$$

B = greater than 4:

$$  
{5,6}  
$$

If you simply add:

$$  
3+2=5  
$$

you counted 6 twice.

Correct:

$$  
|A\cup B|

= 3+2-1

=4  
$$

Outcomes:

$$  
\{2,4,5,6\}  
$$

Therefore:

$$  
P(A\cup B)=\frac46=\frac23  
$$

# Probability + Counting

Most difficult elementary probability questions follow:

$$  
\boxed{  
P(A)=  
\frac{\#\text{valid arrangements/selections}}  
{\#\text{all arrangements/selections}}  
}  
$$

So in many problems, **probability itself is easy**.

The hard part is counting numerator and denominator correctly.

---

# Example — 5 Cards, At Least One Ace

Total hands:

$$  
\binom{52}{5}  
$$

Instead of counting 1 ace + 2 aces + 3 aces + 4 aces:

use complement.

No ace means all five come from 48 non-aces:

$$  
\binom{48}{5}  
$$

Therefore:

$$  
\boxed{  
P(\ge1\text{ ace})
=
1-\frac{\binom{48}{5}}{\binom{52}{5}}  
}  
$$

Exactly 2 aces:

$$  
\binom42  
$$

Remaining 3 must be non-aces:

$$  
\binom{48}{3}  
$$

Therefore:

$$  
\boxed{  
P=  
\frac{\binom42\binom{48}{3}}  
{\binom{52}{5}}  
}  
$$

# Example — At Most One Ace

Means:

- zero aces
    
- one ace.
    

Therefore:

$$ 
\boxed{

\frac{  
\binom{48}{5}  
+  
\binom41\binom{48}{4}

\binom{52}{5}  
}  
}  
$$

This is a perfect illustration of exactly/at-most/at-least.

# Finally: What Should Become Automatic in Your Head?

When you read:

### "Choose"

Think:

$$  
\binom nr  
$$

unless roles/order matter.

### "Arrange"

Think:

$$
{}_nP_r \quad\text{or}\quad n!  
$$

### "Exactly"

Construct that exact case.

### "At least"

Think:

> Should I sum cases, or would complement be easier?

### "At least one"

Your strongest first thought should be:

$$
\boxed{\text{Total}-\text{None}}  
$$

### "At most"

Enumerate:

$$
0,1,\ldots,r  
$$

### "Together"

Create a block.

### "Not together"

$$
\boxed{\text{Total}-\text{Together}}  
$$

### "No two adjacent"

Arrange one group first, then use gaps.

### "Fixed position"

Lock the position before doing anything else.

### "Objects restricted to a group of positions"

Choose/fill those positions separately.

### "Consecutive"

Ask a crucial question:

> Are **specified objects** consecutive?

Then block method.

Or:

> Does there have to be a run of (r) objects of some category?

Then use run/gap/complement methods.

### "OR"

Add, **but subtract overlap if the cases can occur together**.

### "AND"

Construct both requirements, usually multiply.

---

The core skill isn't memorizing twenty formulas. Most counting problems reduce to about **six constructions**:

$$  
\boxed{  
\text{slots},  
\text{choose},  
\text{cases},  
\text{complement},  
\text{blocks},  
\text{gaps}  
}  
$$

If you learn to identify which construction the English sentence is describing, even unfamiliar counting questions become manageable.

# Lecture 5 Study Guide: Conditional Probability, Multiplication Law, Total Probability, and Bayes' Theorem

  

> Based on **Lecture 5**. This note follows the slide topics and examples, then adds closely related ideas that make the methods easier to recognize and apply in exams.  

---

  

## 0. What this lecture is really about

  

The main idea is:

  

> **Probability changes when you receive new information.**

  

If you originally ask for $P(A)$, you are considering the whole sample space. But if you are told that $B$ has already occurred, you are no longer considering the whole sample space. You restrict attention to the part where $B$ is true.

  

That gives **conditional probability**:

  

$$

P(A\mid B)

$$

  

read as:

  

> probability of $A$ **given that** $B$ has occurred.

  

The lecture then builds this chain:

  

$$

\boxed{

\text{Conditional Probability}

\rightarrow

\text{Multiplication Law}

\rightarrow

\text{Total Probability}

\rightarrow

\text{Bayes' Theorem}

}

$$

  

A large number of exam questions are just different wordings of these four ideas.

  

---

  

# 1. Conditional Probability

  

## 1.1 Definition

  

The lecture defines conditional probability as the probability of an event $A$ when it is already known that another event $B$ has occurred.

  

The notation is:

  

$$

\boxed{P(A\mid B)}

$$

  

and the formula is:

  

$$

\boxed{

P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}

}

$$

  

provided $P(B)\neq 0$.

  

Similarly,

  

$$

\boxed{

P(B\mid A)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(A)}

}

$$

  

provided $P(A)\neq 0$.

  

In general,

  

$$

P(A\mid B)\neq P(A)

$$

  

because knowing $B$ may change how likely $A$ is.

  

---

  

# 2. The most important intuition: the event after `|` becomes the new universe

  

This is the easiest way to understand conditional probability.

  

Suppose there are 100 students.

  

- 40 use the bus.

- 10 both use the bus and are late.

  

Let

  

- $B=$ student uses the bus

- $L=$ student is late

  

Without any extra information,

  

$$

P(L\cap B)=\frac{10}{100}

$$

  

But suppose the question asks:

  

> What is the probability that the student is late **given that the student uses the bus**?

  

That is:

  

$$

P(L\mid B)

$$

  

Once we know the student uses the bus, the other 60 students are irrelevant.

  

The new universe has only the 40 bus users.

  

Among those 40, 10 are late.

  

Therefore,

  

$$

P(L\mid B)=\frac{10}{40}=\frac14

$$

  

This is exactly what the formula says:

  

$$

P(L\mid B)

=

\frac{P(L\cap B)}{P(B)}

=

\frac{10/100}{40/100}

=

\frac{10}{40}

$$

  

## Mental rule

  

Whenever you see

  

$$

P(A\mid B)

$$

  

think:

  

> **First restrict the universe to $B$. Then ask what fraction of that restricted universe also belongs to $A$.**

  

The event on the **right side of the bar** tells you the new denominator.

  

---

  

# 3. Conditional probability using counts

  

When all elementary outcomes are equally likely,

  

$$

P(A\mid B)

=

\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}

$$

  

can be written as

  

$$

\boxed{

P(A\mid B)=\frac{n(A\cap B)}{n(B)}

}

$$

  

This is often easier to use in dice, card, and table problems.

  

### Example

  

Roll one die.

  

Let

  

$$

A=\{\text{even}\}=\{2,4,6\}

$$

  

and

  

$$

B=\{\text{number greater than }3\}=\{4,5,6\}

$$

  

Then

  

$$

A\cap B=\{4,6\}

$$

  

So

  

$$

P(A\mid B)

=

\frac{n(A\cap B)}{n(B)}

=

\frac23

$$

  

Notice that we divide by $3$, not $6$, because once $B$ is given, the new universe is

  

$$

\{4,5,6\}.

$$

  

---

  

# 4. Do not reverse the condition

  

A very common mistake is assuming

  

$$

P(A\mid B)=P(B\mid A).

$$

  

Usually this is false.

  

For example:

  

- $D=$ person has a disease

- $+=$ test is positive

  

Then

  

$$

P(+\mid D)

$$

  

means:

  

> Among people who have the disease, how many test positive?

  

But

  

$$

P(D\mid +)

$$

  

means:

  

> Among people who tested positive, how many actually have the disease?

  

Those are completely different populations and therefore different denominators.

  

Bayes' theorem is the tool that connects them.

  

---

  

# 5. Multiplication Law

  

Starting from

  

$$

P(B\mid A)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(A)},

$$

  

multiply both sides by $P(A)$:

  

$$

\boxed{

P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B\mid A)

}

$$

  

Similarly,

  

$$

\boxed{

P(A\cap B)=P(B)P(A\mid B)

}

$$

  

Therefore,

  

$$

\boxed{

P(A\cap B)

=

P(A)P(B\mid A)

=

P(B)P(A\mid B)

}

$$

  

The slides call this the **multiplication law**, **multiplication theorem**, or **law of compound probability**.

  

## Important clarification

  

The slides present this mainly in the context of dependent events. More generally, the multiplication rule itself is valid whenever the relevant conditional probability is defined.

  

If $A$ and $B$ are independent, then

  

$$

P(B\mid A)=P(B)

$$

  

so the general rule simplifies to

  

$$

P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B).

$$

  

So:

  

$$

\boxed{

\text{General: }P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B\mid A)

}

$$

  

$$

\boxed{

\text{Independent special case: }P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)

}

$$

  

---

  

# 6. Why the multiplication law makes intuitive sense

  

Suppose:

  

$$

P(A)=0.40

$$

  

and among the cases where $A$ happens,

  

$$

P(B\mid A)=0.30.

$$

  

Imagine 1000 trials.

  

About

  

$$

1000(0.40)=400

$$

  

will satisfy $A$.

  

Among those 400,

  

$$

400(0.30)=120

$$

  

will also satisfy $B$.

  

Thus

  

$$

P(A\cap B)=\frac{120}{1000}=0.12.

$$

  

And the formula gives

  

$$

0.40\times0.30=0.12.

$$

  

---

  

# 7. Chain Rule for Three Events

  

The lecture extends the multiplication rule to three events:

  

$$

\boxed{

P(A_1\cap A_2\cap A_3)

=

P(A_1)

P(A_2\mid A_1)

P(A_3\mid A_1\cap A_2)

}

$$

  

Interpretation:

  

1. $A_1$ occurs.

2. Given that $A_1$ occurred, $A_2$ occurs.

3. Given that both $A_1$ and $A_2$ occurred, $A_3$ occurs.

  

---

  

# 8. Chain Rule for $k$ Events

  

For events $A_1,A_2,\ldots,A_k$,

  

$$

\boxed{

P(A_1\cap A_2\cap\cdots\cap A_k)

=

P(A_1)

P(A_2\mid A_1)

P(A_3\mid A_1\cap A_2)

\cdots

P(A_k\mid A_1\cap\cdots\cap A_{k-1})

}

$$

  

This is particularly useful for repeated draws without replacement.

  

### Example: three aces without replacement

  

From a standard deck,

  

$$

P(\text{first 3 cards are aces})

=

\frac4{52}\times\frac3{51}\times\frac2{50}.

$$

  

Each probability changes because previous cards are no longer in the deck.

  

---

  

# 9. Tree-diagram way of thinking

  

Many conditional probability and Bayes problems become easier if you imagine a probability tree.

  

Suppose the first stage is $A$ versus $A^c$:

  

- $A$ with probability $P(A)$

  - then $B$ with probability $P(B\mid A)$

  - then $B^c$ with probability $P(B^c\mid A)$

- $A^c$ with probability $P(A^c)$

  - then $B$ with probability $P(B\mid A^c)$

  - then $B^c$ with probability $P(B^c\mid A^c)$

  

A full path is found by **multiplying**.

  

For example,

  

$$

P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B\mid A).

$$

  

If the same final event can occur through several mutually exclusive paths, **add** the path probabilities.

  

For example,

  

$$

P(B)

=

P(A)P(B\mid A)

+

P(A^c)P(B\mid A^c).

$$

  

That second idea is the **law of total probability**.

# 10. Slide Problem 1: Two Dice

  

A pair of dice is thrown.

  

Find the probability that the sum is 10 or greater **given that the first die shows 5**.

  

Let

  

$$

A=\{\text{sum is at least }10\}

$$

  

and

  

$$

B=\{\text{first die is }5\}.

$$

  

The slide lists

  

$$

B=\{(5,1),(5,2),(5,3),(5,4),(5,5),(5,6)\}.

$$

  

To have total at least 10 while the first die is 5, the second die must be 5 or 6.

  

Thus

  

$$

A\cap B=\{(5,5),(5,6)\}.

$$

  

Therefore,

  

$$

P(A\mid B)

=

\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}

=

\frac{2/36}{6/36}

=

\boxed{\frac13}.

$$

  

## Faster intuitive solution

  

Once the first die is known to be 5, forget all outcomes where the first die is not 5.

  

The second die can be

  

$$

1,2,3,4,5,6.

$$

  

To make the total at least 10, it must be

  

$$

5\text{ or }6.

$$

  

Hence

  

$$

\frac26=\boxed{\frac13}.

$$

  

This is the **restricted sample-space** interpretation of conditional probability.

# 11. Slide Problem 2: Husband and Wife Watching TV

  

Let

  

- $H=$ husband watches the show

- $W=$ wife watches the show

  

Given:

  

$$

P(H)=0.4,

\qquad

P(W)=0.5,

\qquad

P(H\mid W)=0.7.

$$

  

## (a) Probability that both watch

  

Use the multiplication law:

  

$$

P(H\cap W)

=

P(W)P(H\mid W)

$$

  

$$

=0.5\times0.7

$$

  

$$

\boxed{P(H\cap W)=0.35}

$$

  

---

  

## (b) Probability wife watches given husband watches

  

We want

  

$$

P(W\mid H).

$$

  

Use

  

$$

P(W\mid H)

=

\frac{P(W\cap H)}{P(H)}.

$$

  

Thus

  

$$

P(W\mid H)

=

\frac{0.35}{0.40}

=

\boxed{0.875}.

$$

  

Notice again:

  

$$

P(H\mid W)=0.7

$$

  

but

  

$$

P(W\mid H)=0.875.

$$

  

They are not the same quantity.

  

---

  

## (c) Probability at least one watches

  

“At least one” means union:

  

$$

P(H\cup W)

=

P(H)+P(W)-P(H\cap W).

$$

  

Therefore,

  

$$

P(H\cup W)

=

0.4+0.5-0.35

=

\boxed{0.55}.

$$

  

---

  

# 12. Slide Problem 3: Toss Until Head or Three Tosses

  

A coin is tossed until either:

  

1. a head appears, or

2. the coin has been tossed three times.

  

The sample space is

  

$$

S=\{H,TH,TTH,TTT\}.

$$

  

These outcomes are **not equally likely**.

  

Their probabilities are

  

$$

P(H)=\frac12,

$$

  

$$

P(TH)=\frac14,

$$

  

$$

P(TTH)=\frac18,

$$

  

$$

P(TTT)=\frac18.

$$

  

The question asks:

  

> Given that no head occurs on the first toss, what is the probability that the coin is tossed three times?

  

Let

  

$$

A=\{\text{coin is tossed three times}\}

=\{TTH,TTT\},

$$

  

and

  

$$

B=\{\text{first toss is not head}\}

=\{TH,TTH,TTT\}.

$$

  

Then

  

$$

P(A\cap B)=P(A)=\frac18+\frac18=\frac14

$$

  

and

  

$$

P(B)=\frac14+\frac18+\frac18=\frac12.

$$

  

Therefore,

  

$$

P(A\mid B)

=

\frac{1/4}{1/2}

=

\boxed{\frac12}.

$$

  

## Faster intuition

  

We are already told the first toss was tail.

  

For the experiment to continue to the third toss, the **second toss must also be tail**.

  

That has probability

  

$$

\boxed{\frac12}.

$$

  

The result of the third toss does not matter because once the third toss is reached, the experiment stops anyway.

  

## Important lesson

  

Do not blindly use

  

$$

\frac{\text{number of favorable outcomes}}{\text{number of total outcomes}}

$$

  

unless the outcomes being counted are equally likely.

  

Here, $H$, $TH$, $TTH$, and $TTT$ do not have equal probabilities.

# 13. Slide Problem 4: Disability Given Male

  

A community has equal numbers of males and females.

  

- $5\%$ of males are disabled.

- $2\%$ of females are disabled.

  

Let

  

- $M=$ male

- $F=$ female

- $D=$ disabled

  

Since males and females are equally common,

  

$$

P(M)=P(F)=0.5.

$$

  

The wording “5% of males are disabled” directly means

  

$$

\boxed{P(D\mid M)=0.05}.

$$

  

We can verify using the conditional formula.

  

First,

  

$$

P(M\cap D)

=

P(M)P(D\mid M)

=

0.5(0.05)

=

0.025.

$$

  

Then

  

$$

P(D\mid M)

=

\frac{P(D\cap M)}{P(M)}

=

\frac{0.025}{0.5}

=

\boxed{0.05}.

$$

  

> **Slide note:** the displayed intermediate arithmetic on this problem appears inconsistent in one line. The correct numerator is $0.025$, giving $0.025/0.5=0.05$. The final answer $0.05$ is correct.

  

## Very important wording recognition

  

“5% **of males** are disabled” already means

  

$$

P(D\mid M)=0.05.

$$

  

Likewise:

  

- “10% of bus users are late” means $P(L\mid B)=0.10$.

- “3% of Machine A products are defective” means $P(D\mid A)=0.03$.

- “90% of diseased people test positive” means $P(+\mid D)=0.90$.

  

The phrase **“of group X”** often creates a conditional probability with $X$ after the bar.

# 14. Law of Total Probability

  

This idea is used directly in Bayes problems.

  

Suppose every outcome belongs to exactly one of the groups

  

$$

A_1,A_2,\ldots,A_k.

$$

  

The groups must form a **partition** of the sample space:

  

1. they do not overlap,

2. together they cover the entire sample space.

  

Then any event $B$ can occur through one of these groups:

  

$$

B=(B\cap A_1)\cup(B\cap A_2)\cup\cdots\cup(B\cap A_k).

$$

  

Since the pieces are mutually exclusive,

  

$$

P(B)

=

P(B\cap A_1)+P(B\cap A_2)+\cdots+P(B\cap A_k).

$$

  

Using the multiplication rule,

  

$$

P(B\cap A_i)=P(A_i)P(B\mid A_i).

$$

  

Therefore,

  

$$

\boxed{

P(B)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}P(A_i)P(B\mid A_i)

}

$$

  

For two groups $A$ and $A^c$:

  

$$

\boxed{

P(B)

=

P(A)P(B\mid A)

+

P(A^c)P(B\mid A^c)

}

$$

  

---

  

# 15. Why total probability is a weighted average

  

Suppose:

  

- 40% of products come from Factory A, with 5% defective.

- 60% come from Factory B, with 10% defective.

  

Then the overall defective rate is not

  

$$

\frac{5\%+10\%}{2}=7.5\%

$$

  

because the factories do not produce equal amounts.

  

Instead,

  

$$

P(D)

=

0.4(0.05)+0.6(0.10)

$$

  

$$

=0.02+0.06

$$

  

$$

=\boxed{0.08}.

$$

  

This is a **weighted average**, where the weights are the group probabilities.

# 16. Bayes' Theorem

  

Bayes' theorem lets us reverse a conditional probability.

  

Suppose we know

  

$$

P(B\mid A)

$$

  

but want

  

$$

P(A\mid B).

$$

  

Start from conditional probability:

  

$$

P(A\mid B)

=

\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}.

$$

  

Using

  

$$

P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B\mid A),

$$

  

we get

  

$$

P(A\mid B)

=

\frac{P(A)P(B\mid A)}{P(B)}.

$$

  

If the sample space is split into $A$ and $A^c$, then

  

$$

P(B)

=

P(A)P(B\mid A)

+

P(A^c)P(B\mid A^c).

$$

  

Therefore,

  

$$

\boxed{

P(A\mid B)

=

\frac{P(A)P(B\mid A)}

{P(A)P(B\mid A)+P(A^c)P(B\mid A^c)}

}

$$

  

---

  

# 17. General Bayes' theorem for many possible sources

  

Suppose $A_1,A_2,\ldots,A_k$ form a partition of the sample space.

  

If event $B$ occurs and we want the probability that source $A_i$ was responsible, then

  

$$

\boxed{

P(A_i\mid B)

=

\frac{P(A_i)P(B\mid A_i)}

{\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{k}P(A_j)P(B\mid A_j)}

}

$$

  

This is the general form used in problems involving:

  

- several machines,

- several factories,

- several hospitals,

- several transport methods,

- several study methods,

- several boxes or containers,

- several diagnostic methods.

  

---

  

# 18. Bayes terminology that is useful to know

  

This terminology is not essential for computation, but it helps organize the idea.

  

In

  

$$

P(A\mid B)

=

\frac{P(B\mid A)P(A)}{P(B)},

$$

  

we can call:

  

- $P(A)$ = **prior probability**

- $P(B\mid A)$ = **likelihood**

- $P(B)$ = **evidence** or total probability of the observed event

- $P(A\mid B)$ = **posterior probability**

  

Bayes' theorem can therefore be remembered conceptually as

  

$$

\boxed{

\text{Posterior}

\propto

\text{Likelihood}\times\text{Prior}

}

$$

  

with the denominator $P(B)$ normalizing the result so the posterior probabilities add to 1.

# 19. Bayes as “reverse direction”

  

Suppose:

  

$$

P(+\mid D)=0.90.

$$

  

This says:

  

> If a person has the disease, there is a 90% chance of a positive test.

  

But the question may ask:

  

$$

P(D\mid +).

$$

  

That asks:

  

> If a person tested positive, what is the probability that the person actually has the disease?

  

Bayes reverses the direction:

  

$$

\boxed{

P(+\mid D)

\longrightarrow

P(D\mid +)

}

$$

  

but it cannot do so using $P(+\mid D)$ alone. We also need the **base rate** $P(D)$ and information about positive tests among people without the disease.

# 20. Slide Bayes Problem 1: Medical Test

  

Given:

  

- disease prevalence:

  

$$

P(D)=0.01

$$

  

- sensitivity:

  

$$

P(+\mid D)=0.90

$$

  

- false-positive rate:

  

$$

P(+\mid D^c)=0.05

$$

  

Thus

  

$$

P(D^c)=0.99.

$$

  

We want

  

$$

P(D\mid +).

$$

  

Using Bayes:

  

$$

P(D\mid +)

=

\frac{P(D)P(+\mid D)}

{P(D)P(+\mid D)+P(D^c)P(+\mid D^c)}.

$$

  

Substitute:

  

$$

P(D\mid +)

=

\frac{0.01(0.90)}

{0.01(0.90)+0.99(0.05)}.

$$

  

Numerator:

  

$$

0.01(0.90)=0.009.

$$

  

Denominator:

  

$$

0.009+0.0495=0.0585.

$$

  

Therefore,

  

$$

P(D\mid +)

=

\frac{0.009}{0.0585}

\approx

0.153846.

$$

  

So

  

$$

\boxed{P(D\mid +)\approx 0.154}

$$

  

or about

  

$$

\boxed{15.4\%}.

$$

  

The slide rounds this to approximately $0.15$.

# 21. Why the medical-test result is only about 15%

  

At first this can feel strange because the test detects 90% of diseased people.

  

Use 10,000 people.

  

### Diseased

  

$$

1\%\text{ of }10,000=100.

$$

  

Of these, 90% test positive:

  

$$

100(0.90)=90.

$$

  

### Healthy

  

$$

9900

$$

  

are healthy.

  

5% of healthy people test positive:

  

$$

9900(0.05)=495.

$$

  

### Total positives

  

$$

90+495=585.

$$

  

Only 90 of those 585 positive tests are actually diseased:

  

$$

\frac{90}{585}\approx0.154.

$$

  

The disease is rare, so even a relatively small false-positive rate creates many false positives.

  

This is called the **base-rate effect** and is one of the most important intuitions behind Bayes' theorem.

# 22. Slide Bayes Problem 2: Two Containers

  

Container 1 contains:

  

- 2 white

- 7 black

  

so

  

$$

P(W\mid H)=\frac29

$$

  

if heads selects the first container.

  

Container 2 contains:

  

- 5 white

- 6 black

  

so

  

$$

P(W\mid T)=\frac5{11}.

$$

  

The coin is fair:

  

$$

P(H)=P(T)=\frac12.

$$

  

We want:

  

$$

P(H\mid W).

$$

  

Use Bayes:

  

$$

P(H\mid W)

=

\frac{P(H)P(W\mid H)}

{P(H)P(W\mid H)+P(T)P(W\mid T)}.

$$

  

Substitute:

  

$$

P(H\mid W)

=

\frac{\frac12\cdot\frac29}

{\frac12\cdot\frac29+\frac12\cdot\frac5{11}}.

$$

  

Cancel the common factor $1/2$:

  

$$

P(H\mid W)

=

\frac{\frac29}

{\frac29+\frac5{11}}.

$$

  

Use a common denominator:

  

$$

\frac29=\frac{22}{99},

\qquad

\frac5{11}=\frac{45}{99}.

$$

  

Thus

  

$$

P(H\mid W)

=

\frac{22}{22+45}

=

\boxed{\frac{22}{67}}.

$$

# 23. Slide Practice Problem: Transport and Being Late

  

A person goes to work using:

  

- car 30% of the time,

- walking 30% of the time,

- bus 40% of the time.

  

Let

  

- $C=$ car

- $W=$ walk

- $B=$ bus

- $L=$ late

  

Given:

  

$$

P(C)=0.30,

\qquad

P(W)=0.30,

\qquad

P(B)=0.40.

$$

  

Also,

  

$$

P(L\mid W)=0.10,

$$

  

$$

P(L\mid C)=0.03,

$$

  

$$

P(L\mid B)=0.07.

$$

  

---

  

## (i) Probability that he is late

  

Use total probability:

  

$$

P(L)

=

P(C)P(L\mid C)

+

P(W)P(L\mid W)

+

P(B)P(L\mid B).

$$

  

Therefore,

  

$$

P(L)

=

0.30(0.03)+0.30(0.10)+0.40(0.07).

$$

  

$$

=0.009+0.030+0.028

$$

  

$$

\boxed{P(L)=0.067}.

$$

  

So the person is late about

  

$$

\boxed{6.7\%}

$$

  

of the time.

  

---

  

## (ii) If he is late, probability he took the bus

  

We want

  

$$

P(B\mid L).

$$

  

Bayes:

  

$$

P(B\mid L)

=

\frac{P(B)P(L\mid B)}{P(L)}.

$$

  

$$

=

\frac{0.40(0.07)}{0.067}

$$

  

$$

=

\frac{0.028}{0.067}

=

\boxed{\frac{28}{67}}

\approx

\boxed{0.418}.

$$

  

---

  

## (iii) If he is late, probability he took the car

  

$$

P(C\mid L)

=

\frac{P(C)P(L\mid C)}{P(L)}

$$

  

$$

=

\frac{0.30(0.03)}{0.067}

$$

  

$$

=

\frac{0.009}{0.067}

=

\boxed{\frac9{67}}

\approx

\boxed{0.134}.

$$

  

### Extra check

  

The remaining posterior probability is walking:

  

$$

P(W\mid L)

=

\frac{0.30(0.10)}{0.067}

=

\frac{30}{67}.

$$

  

And indeed,

  

$$

\frac{28}{67}+\frac9{67}+\frac{30}{67}=1.

$$

# 27. How to recognize which formula to use

  

## Case A: The question literally says “given that”

  

Example:

  

> Probability that a person is late **given that** they took the bus.

  

Immediately write:

  

$$

P(L\mid B).

$$

  

Then usually use

  

$$

P(L\mid B)=\frac{P(L\cap B)}{P(B)}.

$$

  

---

  

## Case B: You want “A and B”

  

Write:

  

$$

P(A\cap B).

$$

  

If conditional information is available:

  

$$

P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B\mid A).

$$

  

If independent:

  

$$

P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B).

$$

  

---

  

## Case C: One final event can arise from several different sources

  

Examples:

  

- late via car/walk/bus,

- defective via Machine A/B/C,

- positive via Center A/B/C,

- pass via books/videos/group study.

  

Use **total probability**:

  

$$

P(B)=\sum_iP(A_i)P(B\mid A_i).

$$

  

---

  

## Case D: The final event happened and you are asked which source it came from

  

Examples:

  

> The person was late. What is the probability they took the bus?

  

> The item is defective. What is the probability it came from Machine B?

  

> The test is positive. What is the probability the person actually has the disease?

  

This is a **reverse conditional** problem.

  

Use Bayes:

  

$$

P(A_i\mid B)

=

\frac{P(A_i)P(B\mid A_i)}

{\sum_j P(A_j)P(B\mid A_j)}.

$$

  

---

  

# 28. The best exam methodology for Bayes problems

  

Do not start by memorizing a giant Bayes formula.

  

Use this four-step method.

  

## Step 1: Define the possible sources

  

Example:

  

$$

C=\text{car},\quad W=\text{walk},\quad B=\text{bus}.

$$

  

## Step 2: Calculate each complete path

  

For example:

  

$$

P(C\cap L)=P(C)P(L\mid C).

$$

  

$$

P(W\cap L)=P(W)P(L\mid W).

$$

  

$$

P(B\cap L)=P(B)P(L\mid B).

$$

  

## Step 3: Add the relevant paths to get the observed event

  

$$

P(L)

=

P(C\cap L)+P(W\cap L)+P(B\cap L).

$$

  

## Step 4: Divide the desired path by all observed-event paths

  

If we want bus given late:

  

$$

P(B\mid L)

=

\frac{P(B\cap L)}{P(L)}.

$$

  

This method is Bayes' theorem, but it is often easier to remember than the formula itself.

# 29. “Forward” vs “reverse” probability

  

This distinction is extremely useful.

  

Suppose:

  

$$

P(L\mid B)=0.07.

$$

  

This is a **forward probability**:

  

> If I know the transport method, how likely is lateness?

  

But

  

$$

P(B\mid L)

$$

  

is the reverse question:

  

> If I observe lateness, how likely is the bus to have been used?

  

Bayes' theorem is mainly needed when going from

  

$$

\boxed{P(\text{effect}\mid\text{cause})}

$$

  

to

  

$$

\boxed{P(\text{cause}\mid\text{effect})}.

$$

  

The words “cause” and “effect” are only intuition; conditional probability itself does not require literal causation.

  

---

  

# 30. Conditional probability does not necessarily mean chronological order

  

The notation

  

$$

P(A\mid B)

$$

  

means that $B$ is **known**, not necessarily that $B$ happened physically before $A$.

  

For example:

  

$$

P(\text{male}\mid\text{brown eyes})

$$

  

is perfectly meaningful even though neither property “happened first.”

  

Conditioning is fundamentally about **information**, not time.

# 31. Independence and conditional probability

  

Events $A$ and $B$ are independent if learning that one occurred does not change the probability of the other.

  

Equivalent conditions include:

  

$$

\boxed{P(A\mid B)=P(A)}

$$

  

provided $P(B)>0$,

  

or

  

$$

\boxed{P(B\mid A)=P(B)}

$$

  

provided $P(A)>0$,

  

or

  

$$

\boxed{P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)}.

$$

  

### Example

  

Suppose

  

$$

P(A)=0.4,

\qquad

P(B)=0.5,

\qquad

P(A\cap B)=0.2.

$$

  

Then

  

$$

P(A)P(B)=0.4(0.5)=0.2.

$$

  

Thus $A$ and $B$ are independent.

  

Also,

  

$$

P(A\mid B)

=

\frac{0.2}{0.5}

=0.4

=P(A).

$$

  

---

  

# 32. Independence vs mutually exclusive events

  

These are very different concepts.

  

## Mutually exclusive

  

$A$ and $B$ cannot both happen:

  

$$

P(A\cap B)=0.

$$

  

## Independent

  

One event gives no information about the other:

  

$$

P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B).

$$

  

If $A$ and $B$ are mutually exclusive and both have positive probability, they **cannot be independent**.

  

Why?

  

If $A$ occurs, then $B$ becomes impossible:

  

$$

P(B\mid A)=0.

$$

  

But if $P(B)>0$, then

  

$$

P(B\mid A)\neq P(B).

$$

  

So learning $A$ occurred definitely changes the probability of $B$.

  

---

  

# 33. Conditional complement rule

  

Within the restricted universe $B$, either $A$ happens or $A^c$ happens.

  

Therefore,

  

$$

\boxed{

P(A^c\mid B)=1-P(A\mid B)

}

$$

  

### Example

  

If

  

$$

P(\text{late}\mid\text{bus})=0.07,

$$

  

then

  

$$

P(\text{not late}\mid\text{bus})

=1-0.07

=\boxed{0.93}.

$$

  

---

  

# 34. Conditional union rule

  

Inside a conditioned universe $C$, the ordinary union formula still works:

  

$$

\boxed{

P(A\cup B\mid C)

=

P(A\mid C)

+

P(B\mid C)

-

P(A\cap B\mid C)

}

$$

  

This is not central to the slide, but it is a useful extension.

# 35. Conditional probability from a $2\times2$ table

  

Suppose:

  

| | $B$ | $B^c$ | Total |

|---|---:|---:|---:|

| $A$ | 30 | 20 | 50 |

| $A^c$ | 10 | 40 | 50 |

| Total | 40 | 60 | 100 |

  

Then

  

$$

P(A\mid B)

=

\frac{30}{40}

=\frac34.

$$

  

Why denominator 40?

  

Because **given $B$**, only the $B$ column matters.

  

Similarly,

  

$$

P(B\mid A)

=

\frac{30}{50}

=\frac35.

$$

  

Given $A$, only the $A$ row matters.

  

This is a direct continuation of the joint-probability-table material from the previous lecture.

# 36. Bayes from a table

  

Using the same table:

  

$$

P(A\mid B)=\frac{30}{40}.

$$

  

Bayes gives the same result:

  

$$

P(A\mid B)

=

\frac{P(B\mid A)P(A)}{P(B)}.

$$

  

Here,

  

$$

P(B\mid A)=\frac{30}{50},

$$

  

$$

P(A)=\frac{50}{100},

$$

  

$$

P(B)=\frac{40}{100}.

$$

  

So

  

$$

P(A\mid B)

=

\frac{(30/50)(50/100)}{40/100}

=

\frac{30}{40}.

$$

  

Bayes is not creating a new probability rule out of nowhere. It is essentially reorganizing joint and conditional probabilities.

# 37. Common mistakes

  

## Mistake 1: Reversing conditional probabilities

  

Wrong:

  

$$

P(A\mid B)=P(B\mid A).

$$

  

Correct: treat them as different unless you calculate and discover they happen to be equal.

  

---

  

## Mistake 2: Using the whole sample space after “given”

  

If asked

  

$$

P(A\mid B),

$$

  

the denominator is $P(B)$, not 1.

  

---

  

## Mistake 3: Multiplying ordinary probabilities when dependence exists

  

Wrong in general:

  

$$

P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B).

$$

  

Correct general form:

  

$$

P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B\mid A).

$$

  

Only simplify to $P(A)P(B)$ when independence is justified.

  

---

  

## Mistake 4: Confusing joint and conditional probability

  

“5% of Machine A items are defective” means

  

$$

P(D\mid A)=0.05,

$$

  

not

  

$$

P(A\cap D)=0.05.

$$

  

The joint probability would be

  

$$

P(A\cap D)=P(A)P(D\mid A).

$$

  

---

  

## Mistake 5: Averaging conditional rates without weights

  

If different groups have different sizes, do not simply average their rates.

  

Use

  

$$

P(B)=\sum_iP(A_i)P(B\mid A_i).

$$

  

---

  

## Mistake 6: Bayes denominator includes only the numerator path

  

Wrong:

  

$$

P(A\mid B)=P(A)P(B\mid A).

$$

  

That gives the **joint path**, not the conditional probability.

  

You must divide by the total probability of $B$:

  

$$

P(A\mid B)

=

\frac{P(A)P(B\mid A)}{P(B)}.

$$

  

---

  

## Mistake 7: Forgetting all possible causes in the denominator

  

If $B$ can happen through $A_1,A_2,A_3$, then

  

$$

P(B)

=

P(A_1)P(B\mid A_1)

+

P(A_2)P(B\mid A_2)

+

P(A_3)P(B\mid A_3).

$$

  

Do not omit a possible source.

  

---

  

# 38. One universal Bayes layout

  

When a problem has several categories, make this table before doing algebra:

  

| Source | Prior | Conditional event probability | Joint path |

|---|---:|---:|---:|

| $A_1$ | $P(A_1)$ | $P(B\mid A_1)$ | $P(A_1)P(B\mid A_1)$ |

| $A_2$ | $P(A_2)$ | $P(B\mid A_2)$ | $P(A_2)P(B\mid A_2)$ |

| $A_3$ | $P(A_3)$ | $P(B\mid A_3)$ | $P(A_3)P(B\mid A_3)$ |

  

Then:

  

$$

P(B)=\text{sum of the joint-path column}.

$$

  

And

  

$$

P(A_i\mid B)

=

\frac{\text{desired joint path}}{\text{sum of all joint paths}}.

$$

  

This method is extremely reliable under exam pressure.

and

  

$$

\boxed{P(A\mid B)=P(A)}

$$

  

## Three-event chain rule

  

$$

\boxed{

P(A\cap B\cap C)

=

P(A)P(B\mid A)P(C\mid A\cap B)

}

$$

  

## Total probability

  

If $A_1,\ldots,A_k$ partition the sample space,

  

$$

\boxed{

P(B)=\sum_{i=1}^kP(A_i)P(B\mid A_i)

}

$$

  

## Bayes' theorem

  

$$

\boxed{

P(A_i\mid B)

=

\frac{P(A_i)P(B\mid A_i)}

{\sum_jP(A_j)P(B\mid A_j)}

}

$$

  

## Conditional complement

  

$$

\boxed{

P(A^c\mid B)=1-P(A\mid B)

}

$$

# 46. Phrase-to-method cheat sheet

  

| Question wording | Translate to | Main method |

|---|---|---|

| “A given B” | $P(A\mid B)$ | Conditional probability |

| “A and B” | $P(A\cap B)$ | Multiplication rule |

| “B happens after A” | $P(B\mid A)$ | Conditional probability |

| “Overall probability of B from several groups” | $P(B)$ | Total probability |

| “B happened; which group did it come from?” | $P(A_i\mid B)$ | Bayes |

| “5% of group A…” | $P(\text{event}\mid A)=0.05$ | Read wording as conditional |

| “with replacement” | often independent | Ordinary multiplication may apply |

| “without replacement” | usually dependent | Update denominator / conditional probability |

| “Are A and B independent?” | compare $P(A\cap B)$ with $P(A)P(B)$ | Independence test |

# 47. The shortest possible mental model

  

If you remember nothing else, remember these four ideas.

  

## 1. `Given` changes the denominator

  

$$

P(A\mid B)

=

\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}.

$$

  

Think:

  

> **Only look inside $B$.**

  

---

  

## 2. A complete path is multiplied

  

$$

P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B\mid A).

$$

  

Think:

  

> **Go down one path: multiply.**

  

---

  

## 3. Several possible paths to the same result are added

  

$$

P(B)=\sum_iP(A_i)P(B\mid A_i).

$$

  

Think:

  

> **Same destination through different mutually exclusive routes: add the routes.**

  

---

  

## 4. Bayes asks which route was used after seeing the destination

  

$$

P(A_i\mid B)

=

\frac{P(A_i)P(B\mid A_i)}{P(B)}.

$$

  

Think:

  

> **Desired route / all routes that reach the observed result.**

  

That one interpretation is enough to reconstruct most Bayes problems even if you temporarily forget the full formula.

# 48. Final exam workflow

  

When you read a probability problem:

  

1. **Define the events.**

2. Circle or mentally note phrases such as **given**, **of**, **and**, **overall**, **if observed**, and **from which source**.

3. If there is a `given`, identify the new universe.

4. If you need a joint path, multiply.

5. If one outcome can come from several mutually exclusive sources, calculate every source-to-outcome path and add them.

6. If the outcome is observed and the question asks which source produced it, divide the desired path by the total of all paths: Bayes.

7. Check that probabilities are between 0 and 1.

8. For a set of posterior source probabilities, check that they add to 1.

  

The most important distinction in the lecture is:

  

$$

\boxed{

P(B\mid A)\neq P(A\mid B)\text{ in general}

}

$$

  

and the most useful computational structure is:

  

$$

\boxed{

\text{path probability}

=

\text{source probability}

\times

\text{conditional probability along that source}

}

$$

  

followed by

  

$$

\boxed{

\text{posterior}

=

\frac{\text{desired path}}

{\text{all paths producing the observed event}}

}.

$$
